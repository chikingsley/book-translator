## IN MEMORIAM FERRUCCIO BUSONI

Into smoke and rubble sank what we had known,
encircled by death in dark gigantic arc.
After the dream that flew before us,
our spirit presses on in tempestuous fire.

Far from homeland and motherly land
our ship drifts on uncertain waves.
Laughing boldly we set out—
seafarers we toward undiscovered land.

And when night and horror overcome us,
you sing us free songs of a new homeland—
and all specters float away, a light down.

In rhythm and round the world dissolves brightly,
from stars late happiness trickles down,
glowing radiant shines for us the Kingdom without Space.

## TO FO

When from the sky would not depart
the heavy cloud
and for all people
the sun grew pale,

then from the depths
the new light came near to us,
we slept
and knew: "You are here!"

O suns
from the ground of your eyes!
Springing fountains
of love from your mouth!

Sparkling glow
of your limbs in the ether-sea—
over the waves
you beckon to blazing courage.

Eternal boy,
encircled by the sound of stars!
Giver of refreshment,
roaring and free and beautiful:

Men and women
swing in your light,
drift into death,
to behold you anew.

Always into brightness
calls your white form,
wave upon wave,
never are we old!

## PART ONE: THE MESSAGE

### CHAPTER ONE: SCHIMMELBERG

The inhabitants of the small university town of Schimmelberg still remembered the old ship captain Wilhelm van Lindenhuis quite clearly. His sudden death had caused much talk.

A few days after his gentle wife, who was always somewhat ailing and careworn, had died, some neighbors noticed that old van Lindenhuis no longer took his customary evening walk. But since they saw lights burning in his house the following nights and glimpsed his gaunt figure with its furrowed face at the window now and again, they believed it to be a passing indisposition and did not trouble themselves about it particularly.

One evening, however, two strange boys in storm collars and leather caps came sauntering down the street. They rang at the captain's house and the old man himself opened the door to them, as was unanimously reported by all who happened to be present on the street. According to the assurance of these same witnesses, he recoiled in dismay at the sight of the boys and then let them in without a word. After only a quarter of an hour he opened up for them again, and they strode rapidly away.

The next morning the postman had a letter to deliver to him. Since no one opened when he rang, he assumed the old man was still asleep. But when he returned at midday and then once more toward evening, nothing stirred in the house. He called the neighbors. They consulted briefly on what was to be done and decided to force open the door.

The captain sat dead in his armchair. The doctors determined a heart attack. While searching the house it was noticed that on the desk of his son Melchior, who had been abroad for many years, lay a crown of thorns set with gemstones and an ivory cross. These objects must have been placed there only recently, for they were completely free of dust, while everything else in the room seemed not to have been cleaned for a long time. Former friends of Melchior who were questioned knew nothing of the existence of these two pieces.

Melchior was immediately notified of his father's death in Rome. But all letters and telegrams came back, as he could not be found.

Yet the years passed. And people had other things to think about than old van Lindenhuis and his peculiar son. Only when passing the empty house of the captain did they exchange conjectures now and then about where Melchior might be staying, whether he had learned of the old man's death at all, and why he did not trouble himself about the considerable inheritance that had fallen to him.

A strange person he had always been, they would add, and dug up all the rumors that had once circulated about young van Lindenhuis—above all the story with his two schoolfriends Otto von Lobe and Heinrich Wunderlich, but also his enigmatic relations with Henriette Karlsen, the daughter of the museum director.

Melchior had been about fifteen years old when Otto von Lobe, a slight, gentle boy with a delicate brow and long, pale yellow lashes, entered the school. He quickly befriended him and soon drew him into his circle, to which the young Heinrich Wunderlich, the apothecary's son, also belonged—a reckless fellow with a brown, daring Roman face.

This circle was, as the later investigation by the school authorities established, a kind of mystical secret society. The young people had immersed themselves in old alchemical and Rosicrucian writings and engaged in strange experiments which, according to the statements that Heinrich Wunderlich and others reluctantly made under pressure from the authorities, had the purpose of transforming the human form at will.

Among the chief dogmas of the association was that no poison could harm those who know and are pure. In their boyish presumption, Otto, Melchior, and Heinrich believed, after long efforts, that they had succeeded in finding the composition of a drink that possessed the mystical power to transform one's form according to wish. This drink contained strong poisons.

Each of the three wanted to be the first to taste the magic potion. Since they could not agree, they called the whole society together, informed them that the goal of their striving had been reached, and asked that lots be drawn to decide which of them would be granted the privilege of testing the power of the potion first on his own body.

The comrades were more seized by the romantic horror of the undertaking than concerned with the details of these experiments, which lay exclusively in the hands of Otto, Melchior, and Heinrich. They knew nothing of the poisonous composition of the drink, although the mystical harmlessness of poisons had been discussed among them often enough. Jubilantly they agreed to the proposal. And the lot decided for Otto.

It was resolved to stay together the whole night. With the rising sun Otto was to take the drink. The hours passed amid wild raptures. They outdid one another in painting all the possibilities of transformation. They dreamed of exercising a magical influence over people. They spoke of changing all laws, all customs, all forms of life, of a new, happy humanity in eternal transformation, in which no one would any longer be bound to his accidental form and everyone could appear as whatever he had imagined in his boldest wishes.

At dawn they all set out and went down to the sea. At the outermost tip of the jetty they settled themselves and gazed into the East, which was reddening ever more fiercely.

When the first ray of sun struck him, Otto sprang up and threw off his clothes. With radiant brow he stood in the early light, smiled happily, and slowly drained the cup he had brought.

Within a few minutes he was writhing in death throes.

A strict investigation followed. Melchior was expelled from the school, since he refused to make any statement; the others got off with severe punishments.

A peculiar change came over Heinrich Wunderlich after these events. He shed all the peculiarities of his nature and displayed a cramped, overloud normality. After finishing school he studied medicine at Schimmelberg University, married, and withdrew as a doctor to a small country town, where he dried up into an eccentric in dogged sobriety.

Melchior, on the other hand, whose somewhat adventurously minded and superstitious father made no reproaches and whose mother never showed him her tears, shut himself away, darkened his room, and hardly left it for many weeks. His food was carried up to him.

When he began to show himself again, he spoke long and often with his father, to whom he became ever dearer. For the old man firmly believed that his son would one day realize all the peculiar and wild fantasies that he himself had secretly cherished but had kept hidden from all people, even from his wife.

Thus the two inflamed each other in ever more monstrous conversations and often clashed harshly. For the old man wanted little to do with eternal self-transformation, to which Melchior clung fanatically; he saw no purpose in it in any case. The transformation of form, should one actually be able to achieve it, had in his view only the one meaning: to burn every individual form and shape in its own fire and make it insubstantial, so that the earth might become a pure mirror of the stellar world. When he came to speak of this theme, he lost himself in astrological brooding and hairsplitting that Melchior could not follow.

The old man's thoughts were confused and he expressed them moreover in tangled and unclear ways. Melchior did not quite know where his head was, but felt that everything he longed for was oppressed and violated by this world of his father's, even though it mysteriously attracted him again and again.

He began to contradict. The old man grew heated. This only strengthened Melchior more in his opposition. And so it came about that father and son soon no longer understood each other at all and talked past one another. Nevertheless, the father remained for a long time the only one with whom Melchior spoke seriously. Toward other people he showed himself colder, more distant, calmer than before. Only in his gray, somewhat rigid eyes burned a hidden fire that made him uncanny to many.

Around this time he also resumed his visits to Henriette Karlsen, with whom he had often played before his alchemical period. She had in the meantime become almost grown up. When one looked at her, one believed she must be transparent, so floating, light, and fine was her form. Her hair was white-blond, so that it gave the impression that her amber-yellow eyes had no brows. But the most beautiful thing about her were her tired, long-fingered hands.

Melchior saw her again for the first time when he was still sitting up in his darkened room and one evening looked out through a crack in his window curtain onto the street. The next day he left the room and went to the museum; he knew she could be found there around midday, for she stood in for the old custodian during mealtimes.

She stood before a beautiful copy of Botticelli's Venus that her brother, who had died of consumption some years before, had painted and donated to the museum. When she heard footsteps behind her, she turned around, caught sight of Melchior, and started. Then she went up to him, took him by both hands, and looked at him for a long time. They spoke not a word. But both their eyes suddenly stood full of tears. Melchior turned away and hurried off.

Since that encounter he came to her in the museum every day. What had passed between the two, no one knew. But Henriette grew ever paler and sadder.

One day the old director happened to overhear a conversation between them from the next room, and heard Melchior say to his daughter:

"It is as I told you, Henriette. Every night the face looked in at the window and nodded to me. I had already seen it a few times many years ago, as a small boy still. When I felt very sad and Father was at sea and Mother sat mute, bent over her Bible, I would hear a knocking at the window. Then it was there—a thin, brown face with eyes that were just like mine. But when I ran to the window, it vanished. And I sat for hours and wept. Mother did not know why; I never spoke to her about it. But I told Father. He only smiled strangely and was silent. And now, a year ago, it came again. But not alone—a whole band was with it. While I was making my experiments with the elixir, they often looked in with mocking faces. Since Otto is dead, they have stayed away..."

"Thank God," said Henriette.

"How can you say such a thing?" Melchior interrupted her vehemently. "Since they no longer come, I am completely alone. No one helps me. Otto is dead because we were hasty and did not have the right faith in ourselves. And Heinrich is a traitor. Father—he wants to know nothing of all this, he has something else in mind."

Henriette cried out fearfully:

"If you love me, Melchior, you must promise me to forget all this. Promise me that you will never follow him when he calls you."

"How can I promise that?" Melchior burst out in despair. "How can you ask that of me? I wish for nothing more ardently than to be able to go with him. Then I will solve all the mysteries. And you with me. For you are coming along."

"Never!" screamed Henriette with mortal fear in her voice. "Shall it happen to me as to your friend Otto? Do you want to kill me too?" and she sobbed aloud.

Melchior stamped his foot.

"Shame on you, you are a coward!" he cried indignantly. Weeping choked his voice. "What more do I have to do with you?" and he stormed out of the hall, past the dismayed director, and ran home.

That same day Melchior asked his father to send him to another town so that he could finish his gymnasium there. His father consented.

Melchior left Schimmelberg and came home only rarely for a few days' visit. After he entered university, he stayed away entirely. Through his father, whom people occasionally questioned with curiosity, they learned that he was studying chemistry and astronomy abroad and that he had finally become a doctor at Oxford. In that very year Henriette died of consumption.

Once more, a year before his mother's death, Melchior returned to Schimmelberg, where he stayed three days. During these days father and son were often seen walking up and down the promenade, silent and self-absorbed. Then Melchior departed and went on his travels. Some claimed he had spent a long time in India and China. But no one knew anything more precise.

All these stories and rumors were refreshed from time to time. They received new nourishment when one day a notice appeared in the "Schimmelberger Anzeiger" according to which the young Doctor van Lindenhuis had made a sensational chemical discovery.

Now it happened that the famous chemist at the University of Schimmelberg, Professor Cux, was looking for an assistant. From Melchior's publications he gathered that this man, like no other, was suited for the position. Since Melchior, as he had learned, was staying temporarily in the capital, he got in contact with him. And the result was that Melchior accepted the call of the university. Evidently the prospect of working with Professor Cux appealed to him.

That Melchior was married reassured the agitated citizens of Schimmelberg, for they were a little afraid of him when they thought back to his most peculiar youth. When he then arrived after six years' absence, they were reasonably calmed: he made an odd, cold, and foreign impression, to be sure, but otherwise seemed amiable, sensible, even impressive. Above all, his somewhat exotic-looking wife charmed society.

Professor Cux reported to him on the very first day about his father's death. When he mentioned the two boys, Melchior made a dismayed face, but immediately composed himself again and asked to be shown the crown of thorns and the ivory cross. He shook his head and assured him he did not know the two objects.

"My father often had strange inclinations," he said. "God knows what may have moved him to acquire these things!"

After the old house on the Löwengracht had been newly furnished, things went grandly at Melchior's. The whole town visited him—that is, actually more his wife. He always withdrew soon, excused himself to the company with a cool smile, and retired to his study, where he studied and experimented until deep into the night.

Thus a year passed. Then it struck Melchior's colleagues that he was becoming ever gloomier and more unruly. He neglected his scientific work and took part more often in his wife's social life, which now acquired a completely different character through him. People told each other indignantly of his grotesque mockeries of all state and church institutions. Above all, they were outraged by his ever-growing influence on the student youth, to whom he tried to instill a radical skepticism against all foundations and results of science. The professoriate was indignant. Only the great influence of Professor Cux prevented a public scandal. But Melchior found himself obliged to interrupt his lectures "for reasons of health."

People withdrew from him. Only a few remained loyal, among them Professor Cux, who on occasion was wont to say:

"My dear van Lindenhuis is quite right. What does chemistry matter? And science in general?"

The remark was taken for an indulgent joke by the great scholar, until it became known that he had secretly married a dancer whom he kept locked up at home and introduced to no one. When people learned of this, they made concerned faces and spoke of the baleful influence of a certain circle.

This circle gathered once a week at Melchior's dwelling, delighted in his paradoxes, and gradually came so much under his spell that individual members made themselves extremely unpopular in town through eccentric, even downright absurd actions. Rumor naturally exaggerated these incidents into the monstrous, so that it was soon claimed that shameless orgies were being held at Doctor van Lindenhuis's.

All the more astonished were people when, more recently, Hans Silferharnisk, the well-known liberal preacher at St. Mary's, was counted among Melchior's regular guests and afterward reported everywhere that one gained there an excellent insight into the torn state of the modern soul, and that for him as a pastor this was quite invaluable.

Soon thereafter it was suddenly said that Melchior had completely withdrawn from his circle. He was often seen roaming restlessly through the streets with an absent look. And when in November people claimed to have noticed unknown boys in striking clothing here and there, they remembered the strange circumstances surrounding his father's death, as well as the old museum director Karlsen's account of the conversation that Melchior as a sixteen-year-old had had with Henriette Karlsen. They stood before an insoluble mystery and consequently fell into ever greater agitation, indignation, and suspense.

### CHAPTER TWO: THE ENCOUNTER

Melchior sat peevishly on a bench and stared into the ever more densely falling rain. He could not bring himself to go home. His wife would have deliberately forgotten to have his study heated in order to force him to attend the weekly tea party. So he preferred to freeze outdoors and let the rain soak him through.

Footsteps on the gravel startled him from his apathy. He flinched and stared dumbfounded before him: a boy in a storm collar and leather cap was sauntering along the leafless avenue of the city park. As he came nearer, Melchior perceived a thin, brown face from which gray, somewhat rigid eyes—at once bold and shy—gazed unwaveringly straight ahead.

In passing he turned toward Melchior, looked at him sharply, and smiled at him. He kept carefully to the edge of the path, so that the bushes running along the two bare rows of trees prevented him from being seen from a distance.

Melchior let out a soft cry and began to tremble. Then at the other end of the avenue a tall man appeared, stopped, and looked about uneasily in all directions; then he took a few large, hurried steps, halted again, and looked around once more.

Before this man could have noticed him, the boy ran hastily back to Melchior under cover of the bushes, sat down close beside him on the bench, and whispered to him imploringly:

"Take my left hand! And put on a glove quickly afterward! Be surprised at nothing and tell no one anything about me! Quick, quick..."

The boy's voice sounded so urgent, his eyes had such a feverish gleam, his beautifully curved lips quivered so fearfully, that Melchior involuntarily seized the hand held out to him.

At the same instant the boy had vanished, as though he had dissolved into thin air. But on Melchior's index finger there suddenly sat a broad silver ring.

Before he could give himself an account of what had happened, he mechanically drew his gloves from his coat pocket, still under the impression of the vanished one's anxious plea, and put them on. Only then did the enormity of the incident come to his consciousness and fill him with an oppressive joy, without it being clear to him what exactly he was rejoicing about.

He was himself astonished that he did not lose all composure, all feeling of self-assurance. He knew only: something long hoped for had finally come to pass. All his vexation had departed from him. His figure grew taut. Tensely and almost hostilely he looked toward the man from whom the boy had fled.

When the stranger caught sight of Melchior, he stopped, seemed to ponder something irresolutely, and then took a few hesitant steps toward him. He looked tall and slender. His clean-shaven face, sharply cut and withered, ended in a pointed, energetic chin. The mouth was thin and broad, the nose narrow and curved, the cheeks sunken, and the eyes like bright transparent stones. When he raised his hat, Melchior beheld a mighty brow of radiant white and blond, wavy hair.

"Forgive me," said the stranger, "did you perhaps see a boy pass by here?"

"I paid attention to no one," replied Melchior dismissively.

"Is that so..." said the stranger. "You permit?" And he sat down beside Melchior on the bench. "I am a little tired, have been running about the whole day, searching for my pupil. He got lost in the market crowd this morning."

"What does he look like?" asked Melchior, and had to smile in spite of himself.

The stranger started suspiciously and then said quickly:

"So you did see him after all? Did he speak to you? Did he..."

"I saw no one," Melchior interrupted him. "I already told you so."

"I thought from your question that you remembered something," said the stranger. "One sits sometimes, pays attention to nothing, and yet notices all sorts of things... So you didn't see him... A pity... Forgive my persistence, but I am very worried..."

Melchior scrutinized the man sitting beside him more sharply. The outwardly immobile face of the stranger changed expression from second to second. Sometimes it looked slack and feeble and the eyes gazed blankly. Sometimes a barely perceptible childish smile lay on his lips. Sometimes his features were filled with stern, threatening seriousness and his eyes sparkled cold and piercing.

The stranger rose.

"Excuse me once more," he said. "I have a request of you. I do not know how I come to make it, but I have the feeling that it will be you, precisely you, whom the boy must meet. I know he will speak to you. Do not listen to what he tells you. It is not true. Do not take his hand if he offers it to you—that could turn out badly for you. I warn you. And if you see him, please be so kind as to notify me. Do not refuse me!"

Melchior did not answer.

"My name is Ulrich von Spät," the stranger continued. "I am staying at the Grand Hotel, passing through. You must take me for a madman. At the very least I seem very strange to you. I cannot give you any explanations for my behavior. And yet I ask you to trust me. Indeed, I would almost demand your trust, although I do not know you. There is something in you that seems to give me the right to speak to you in this way. See in me not a stranger, but a human being, a friend, as we should always do when among a thousand indifferent people we believe we recognize a human being... By the way, I forgot—you do not even know yet what the boy looks like: a very thin, sunburned face, gray, motionless eyes, long black hair, wears a storm collar and leather cap... You will certainly recognize him. His appearance must strike anyone..."

At this moment Melchior lowered his head thoughtfully but did not speak a word. Herr von Spät waited a few moments longer, sighed, and looked Melchior full in the face. His eyes seemed darker and more radiant. He extended his hand to Melchior and said:

"So then—hopefully—auf Wiedersehen!"

A wave of heat shot through Melchior. In that instant he felt suddenly a deep inner kinship with the stranger, forgot everything, forgot the ring, stripped off his glove, and shook Herr von Spät warmly by the hand.

The latter caught sight of the ring. A flash passed fleetingly through his eyes, but he concealed his agitation and strode calmly away.

Melchior gazed after him for a long time. Suddenly he started: he noticed the ring on his finger, remembered the boy, and felt like a traitor. Only when it occurred to him that the stranger might not have seen or noticed the ring did he calm down a little, without being able to forgive himself his carelessness.

"What does this mean?" he thought. "I am losing control over myself. Things happen to me as in dreams. Who was the stranger? What power did he have over me that I suddenly loved him, that I could forget who he is? He is my enemy!"

### CHAPTER THREE: FO

On his way home Melchior felt a gliding lightness within himself. Streets, walls, houses enclosed him, tall and strange. They seemed to be shaped from air. It was as if he walked through them. They parted before him like curtains and closed behind him like drifting clouds of mist. Corners around which he had walked day after day rose before him like mysterious structures from a distant, colorful homeland. Everything was transformed. It was no longer the same city through which he walked; it dissolved into shifting visions.

The people too looked transformed. He caught fleeting glances now and then and believed he had looked into his own eyes as into a mirror. Some smile, some gesture of a hand, seemed to him a beckoning, a greeting, a sign of secret understanding.

On the station square, under a gigantic umbrella, sat an old huckster-woman offering apples for sale. He went up to her, bought a few apples, put them in his pocket, and stroked the woman's wrinkled cheeks to her astonishment.

"Yes, yes," he said, beaming, "we know each other, we have always been old friends. Do you see this ring on my hand? You have never seen it before, have you? No one shall see it, except you. It means that I am going away now, very far away. You know how it is when one wants to go very far away, and suddenly the time has come and one goes."

The woman seemed to understand nothing and looked at him fearfully.

"I know," he continued, "that I do not need to tell you any of this at all. We know each other so well, we have known each other for a long time, since my childhood..."

The huckster-woman had grown more and more anxious. She looked around on all sides. Finally she pulled herself together and interrupted Melchior:

"Aren't you ashamed to play stupid jokes on an old woman?"

"You don't know me?" replied Melchior. "Why do you suddenly not want to know me? You always sat at some corner when I was on the road. I always saw you when I left some place or arrived somewhere. Don't you remember how you sat at the station in Genoa with a bright parrot on your shoulder, and I bought oranges from you when I arrived? And in Vienna? In Petersburg? In Stockholm? In a hundred other cities? You were always there and greeted me with your fruits when I arrived, watched me when I departed."

"There must be some mistake, dear sir," said the huckster-woman mistrustfully. "I have never gotten away from here."

Melchior looked her straight in the eyes, shook his head, and finally said in a lowered voice:

"I understand. You are being careful. You don't want to be overheard. The stranger is here—our enemy. It was careless of me to speak to you. We may already have been watched. I was just so happy to see you. Now I know I am going away."

At that moment he saw a boy pass by the huckster-woman's stand who looked at him sharply, put his finger warningly to his lips, and quickly turned the corner. Melchior felt hastily for his ring. It still sat on his finger. Besides, it could not have been the one who vanished. That one's face was thinner, more sunburned, bolder. Only the gray, motionless eyes resembled his.

Melchior nodded a farewell to the huckster-woman and strode rapidly away.

"Who was the one who warned me?" he thought. "He wore the same clothing as the one who vanished. What circle have I entered? What surrounds and captivates me? I have seen all this sometime in a dream. The many familiar faces on the street, this winking, nodding, greeting, the two boys, the stranger... But I cannot remember... And the huckster-woman... Why did I say all that to her? It is madness. How should she know me? Old huckster-women sit at every station. And yet—it was the same face, the same hair, the same wrinkles, the same voice..."

As he approached his dwelling, Melchior perceived in the deep twilight a crowd of boyish figures who scattered at his coming, hid behind the corners of the house, and peeped out curiously.

"It is getting more and more confused," he thought. "Now there are whole bands of them."

The windows of his dwelling, which was on the ground floor, were brightly lit. One heard laughter, chatter, music. Against the lowered white curtains fleeting shadows sometimes stood out. In the dull murmur of voices he believed he heard the clear, bright voice of Herr von Spät. But then it occurred to him that he had not given him his name or address at all, so it must be a deception of his agitated senses.

So as not to be seen, he entered the dwelling through the back door and went straight to his study. There it was dark and cold. He turned a knob on the wall and a blinding brightness spread out. Then he lay down on the divan in his damp coat and closed his eyes. His hand fell slackly over the edge of the divan, so that the ring, which sat only loosely on his finger, slipped to the floor. He started and looked up—

The vanished boy stood beside the divan and looked at him, smiling.

"You are cold," said the boy. "I will make a fire!"

And he knelt at the stove, stacked the wood, and breathed upon it. A flame shot up. The wood crackled.

The boy threw off his collar and cap and stood before Melchior in a close-fitting garment.

"I knew I would find you, Melchior," he said. "I had seen in your eyes that you would help me. You belong to us, even if you know nothing of us yet. I thank you. We all thank you."

"Who are you? Who are all of you?" asked Melchior. "I understand nothing of all that has happened. Who is the stranger? How do you know my name?"

"I have known of you for a long time. I am called Fo. I cannot tell you my true name. None of us may utter his. We give ourselves indifferent nicknames so that we can address one another. Who are we? You will learn that when you live with us. You need only to call: 'I want away!' And we come and fetch you. But beware of the stranger. He is our worst enemy. He saw the ring on your finger and will pursue you. He possesses a secret that makes him powerful. I was in his power and was only able to free myself with much cunning. How—I will tell you when you come to us. You still live among the others; I cannot tell you anything yet. And now—thank you, and let me go free. Our people are waiting for me."

Melchior heard a rustling at the window. Many faces pressed against the panes and looked from the darkness outside into the bright room.

"I will not let you go," cried Melchior, "until you have told me everything. Do I know whether you will come back if I call? How can I follow you if you do not tell me who you are? How am I to resist the stranger if I do not know his secret?"

"Who we are can only be lived, not told. You will follow us if your heart drives you. We are always there when we are called. We ourselves do not know the stranger's secret—otherwise he could do nothing to us. I gave you an answer. Now let me go!"

"You want to escape me!" said Melchior. "But I know how to bind you. I take your hand and transform you back into the ring!"

"The ring is of no use to you, Melchior," laughed the boy. "It dissolves your life into mystery and confusion. Everything transforms for you. Yet you do not move from the spot. The city will always be to you as it was on the way home today. You will unravel nothing, will take friends for enemies and enemies for friends. For you are not able to interpret the signs that reveal themselves to you. Come with us—and you will be free. Call us when you long for us. Until then—let me go! Open the window!"

Melchior hesitated still. Then he stood up silently, looked at Fo for a long time, and opened the window for him. With a bound the boy leaped out. The band outside encircled him. They took one another by the hands. A flame shot up from their midst, scattered into sparks, and all had vanished.

### CHAPTER FOUR: THE UNLEASHED

There was a knock. Melchior did not answer. The door was cautiously opened. His wife Sophie looked in. Small and fragile, with black, smooth hair parted in the middle, she stood in the doorframe and looked at Melchior with her blue-green eyes. Her full, somewhat shapeless lips twitched almost imperceptibly.

"I thought you would already be here," she said. "Why do you stay alone in the cold room? Come over to us. It is very interesting today."

"You know I have nothing to do with those people," he replied. "Why didn't you have my room heated?"

"Forgive me, I forgot," Sophie answered coolly.

"You always forget when you have company."

She straightened up and looked at him resolutely.

"What do you mean by that?" she asked.

"That you want to connect me by every means with people who hold me back. I have no time for them." Melchior rose and paced back and forth.

"Then you have no time for me either," said Sophie, "for with these people I can speak humanly, and that bores you."

Melchior stopped and replied firmly:

"Yes, it bores me. It is always the same discussing and chewing over of sufficiently known things. You all taste lustfully at everything and can therefore always sample the same things again."

A malicious expression flitted across the woman's features. But she controlled herself and said calmly:

"Only in things familiar to me do I feel at home and secure. But you cannot bear it when I feel secure. You would like to see me insecure. As you make every person insecure whom you come in contact with. You are not well when someone knows what he wants. You always try to pull the ground out from under his feet. Since they have known you, they have become quite stupid. One cannot have a serious conversation anymore. It always becomes wild and crazy at once. The most sensible and clear people talk nonsense."

"You do not want to understand me," Melchior interjected. "You are secure, after all. I can only tell you that your security is a deceptive one, just as the former security of your people was a self-deception. The slightest thing can shake it and make it unsound. Only one who no longer knew which way to turn, only one who no longer knew up from down, only one who no longer knew anything of himself—he may speak of security. I trust no firmness, no form, no permanence, no security."

Sophie made an impatient gesture.

"We must not keep the guests waiting any longer," she said. "Do come along. Everything is topsy-turvy today. Someone is there who frightens them even more than you do. He speaks such strange words, as if he has only to command, and a whole army of spirits would obey him."

Melchior smiled.

"Of spirits?" he asked. "So you are more inclined to believe in spirits than in the ghostliness of the whole world?... Who is this conjurer of spirits?"

"An old acquaintance of mine," answered Sophie, embarrassed. "From my hometown. We played together as children. Everyone always had to obey him and was not allowed to play as they wished. He was thin and weak, but no one dared rebel against him. I left home early and have heard nothing of him since. Quite unexpectedly he paid me a visit today, after fifteen years. So I invited him to stay for tea."

"What is his name?"

"Ulrich von Spät."

"Passing through? Staying at the Grand Hotel?" asked Melchior hastily.

"How do you know that? Do you know him?"

"I happened to make his acquaintance a few hours ago, but did not tell him my name. And yet! He must have known it. He can only have come on my account. He used the fact that he knew you. Now I understand."

Melchior had grown more and more agitated. His eyes glowed. Sophie looked at him mockingly.

"All at once you have become quite alive," she said drawlingly. "You are fabricating a new adventure for yourself. So I can still offer you something by roundabout ways, by bringing crazy strangers into the house."

"Come!" Melchior interrupted her gruffly and hurried out into the corridor, toward the salon. Sophie followed him hesitantly.

Before the salon door he paused. He heard a thunderous laugh and immediately afterward the sharp voice of Herr von Spät.

"But my esteemed ladies and gentlemen," he heard Herr von Spät say, "you are only laughing because you want to deceive yourselves about the things I have asserted. I can perform before your eyes what I have just told you. It is no fairy tale, what you know from a hundred fairy tales and stories: I can actually lock each one of you into the little flask I hold here in my hand."

The hooting and laughter of the guests intensified. Most distinctly the shrill bleating of a woman's voice and the fat, hissing snort of a deep bass rang out from the noise.

Melchior opened the door and entered with his wife. He was immediately surrounded and greeted warmly. Astonished, he looked about him. All faces were excited and glowed as if in fever. In most eyes lay a shimmering gleam. Mouths twitched uncontrollably. Nostrils quivered.

"Has Herr von Spät done all this?" thought Melchior, and looked toward him. Herr von Spät stood somewhat apart, leaning against the wall, and looked as if frozen.

"Hello!" shouted Heinrich Trümpelsteg, the fat art critic of the "Evening News," and slapped Melchior roughly on the shoulder. "Hello, old boy, how goes it, most esteemed castle-in-the-clouds dweller? Does one see you again, you soap bubble of our lustful dreams? You come just at the right time. Your famous friend there wants to show us a few tricks."

But Professor Cux, who had meanwhile cleaned his golden spectacles, sticky with drops of sweat, and set them up on his multi-lumped nose, pushed him aside and shook Melchior's hand vigorously.

"Good day, dear young friend, good day," he lisped. "May I introduce my dear wife to you?" And he pulled a boyish-looking woman with short blond hair out from behind his back. "Doesn't she look splendid?"

The young woman blushed defiantly.

"You need not blush, Franziska," Professor Cux continued. "Here it is the custom to say at once whatever one thinks. Just look, dear friend"—he turned to Melchior and lifted his wife's skirt above her knees—"just look at these calves. When I think of these calves, I immediately find the solution to my most complicated chemical formulas. And the actual sight of them positively inspires me to new discoveries—naturally only in the field of chemistry."

The company neighed at this joke. Loudest of all Frau Cux herself. Only now did Melchior see that she had lilac-painted lips and was powdered green. He could not believe his eyes. The other respectable bourgeois wives were painted too. At the sight of Franziska's calves some of them likewise lifted their skirts, showed their calves, and screamed all at once:

"I too have beautiful calves, I too have beautiful calves! No, mine are more beautiful! No, mine!"

"But, ladies," called Heinrich Trümpelsteg in between, "but, ladies, do just listen! Calves alone certainly don't do it, but rather what is with and around the calves. I propose, ladies, we hold a beauty evening. Let the veils fall, let us admire you in all your beauty and decide by majority vote who is the most beautiful among you. We are the risen Greeks! We want nothing but beauty, beauty, beauty!"

A roar of hurrahs arose. A tumbling confusion of legs, arms, articles of clothing whirled through the air. And in a few minutes all the women stood there naked.

Melchior looked toward Sophie. She too had undressed, was swaying her hips, and looked at him mockingly.

"What is going on here?" thought Melchior. "Is this what I said looks like when it has passed through the heads of these people?"

Frau Cux danced through the hall, beat her small firm breasts with her fists, threw herself around the neck of now this one, now that one, and kissed him suckingly. That was the signal for a general dissolution of the women. Shrieking, they darted to and fro, fell to the ground, struck, scratched, bit one another, tore at one another's hair, kissed each other, laughed and scolded. The men, foam on their lips, clapped their applause.

Melchior turned away and approached Herr von Spät, who still stood motionless. The latter came to meet him and extended his hand.

"So we meet sooner after all than we had thought," he said, and looked at Melchior penetratingly for half a second. "What a strange coincidence that it should be precisely you who is the husband of my childhood friend?"

"I do not believe in coincidences," answered Melchior, returning his gaze. "One way or another, we ourselves bring about the coincidences."

While he spoke this worn-out phrase almost thoughtlessly, it passed through his mind that at this moment it had a quite specific, unambiguous meaning, of which only he and Herr von Spät were aware.

"Hurrah for philosophy, Melchior!" cried Trümpelsteg, who had heard the last words. "Hurrah for philosophy, especially in view of so much naked flesh. Ladies, it is in vain—even if you exerted yourselves still more—here philosophy is being practiced. Long live the spirit!"

Trümpelsteg had spoken so loudly that everyone fell silent and listened in amazement.

"Coincidence, coincidence!" he continued. "Naturally there is no coincidence for such a magus as you. You make the coincidences yourself, and Herr von Spät conducts a spirit orchestra to accompany them. Ho, ho, ho, ho, ho!"

He doubled up with laughter and paddled about in the air with his ape-like long arms, which were in no way to be reconciled with his corpulence and his short stature.

The whole company gathered around Melchior and Herr von Spät. Hans Silferharnisk, the gaunt, liberal preacher of St. Mary's, with his goggling, mild fish eyes, called in a thin, cutting voice into the general confusion:

"But we are completely forgetting the miracle promised us... Convince us, Sir... what was your esteemed name again?... right, yes, now it comes back to me, God be praised... convince us then, my dear Herr von Spät, of all that you have spoken to us about. Convince us—and we will gladly confess the hastiness of our judgment. Do not forget that we do not live in the Middle Ages! We enlightened, free people of today bow only before facts. Facts, Herr von Spät, facts!"

"Facts!" roared the whole chorus, increasingly excited by the prospect of the coming sensation.

"Facts!" now the gymnasium professor Karl Schulze also joined the conversation, his blond mustache bristling, and he shook his fist threateningly. "Facts!" he said again and struck his starched shirt-front so that it cracked. "Facts alone convince. There are more things in heaven and earth, and so forth. But facts they must be. Otherwise we do not believe in them. Reality, dear sir, iron, tangible reality, as the great age in which we live has taught us!"

"Bravo!" cheered the chorus.

Here Trümpelsteg could contain himself no longer. Blue-red with conviction, he jumped onto the table, raised his ape-arms conjuringly, and shouted into the company:

"But art, ladies and gentlemen! You forget art! Art has nothing to do with facts. Art, ladies and gentlemen, gives us what life withholds from us: true love, almighty passion, of which we are not at all capable in reality. Already the old master Goethe says: 'In the colored reflection we have life!' And then the divine Plato. And in general. For example, the women in such a Renaissance painting—those are not facts, those are ideals, they make one's mouth water. Hurrah for the ideal! Or the love duets in the opera, when the violins stroke one's spinal cord—we cannot love so wildly, no one among us could bear it, those too are not facts, those are ideals. How we love in reality, ladies and gentlemen, is a piggishness, permit me this candor! In art it becomes a temple festival animated by the earth spirit. There we feel depth, eternity, lustful revelation, worldview! And, ladies and gentlemen, if now our esteemed sorcerer, Herr von Spät, so to speak lifts us through his art above the facts and reality, let us be grateful! Let us beware of taking beautiful appearance for facts! Imagine that these things were actual facts—then everything around us would have to collapse, then we would no longer know which way to turn, then our dear Melchior would be right. No, ladies and gentlemen, I repeat, let us beware of facts! Facts make one common! Let us hold to the spirit, that is harmless! Let us be knights of the spirit!"

"Let us be knights of the spirit!" the others cheered.

Sophie had stood mute in the corner with wide-open eyes. The corners of her mouth twitched lustfully and vengefully. Now she slapped her naked thighs with a clap and joined in the general jubilation with gurgling laughter.

Melchior and Herr von Spät looked at each other and smiled. To Melchior it seemed as if a thin veil were settling over the whole picture. The screaming no longer rang so shrilly; the repulsive contorting of limbs, the distorted faces, the sweaty flesh—everything seemed to become more distant, stranger, more foreign to him. Only Herr von Spät did he feel close to and bound to in the depths.

### CHAPTER FIVE: THE MIRACLES

This state lasted only a few minutes. Melchior pulled himself together and came to his senses. Herr von Spät had left the room. The whole picture the company presented had changed. Melchior stood alone at one of the long walls. At the other the guests crowded together and whispered tensely. Melchior saw how anxious and almost hostile glances struck him now and then. Everything was full of evil expectation.

Then the entrance door, which was on the narrow side of the oblong room, opened, and Herr von Spät entered slowly. A blue haze enveloped him, half shimmering veils, half luminous mist, from which his head emerged white. His eyes lay deep in their sockets and were firmly closed. He held his arms stretched far forward. In one hand he carried a curiously twisted flask, in the other a small flashing knife. He seemed to perceive none of those present and approached with rigidly mincing steps the bay window opposite the entrance door, to which two steps led up.

His pacing seemed to have a challenging effect on the guests. The hostile glances that had struck Melchior now turned upon Herr von Spät and became ever more hate-filled.

When he had passed them, Trümpelsteg and Frau Cux, who had communicated by signs, detached themselves from the group and crept after him cautiously and crouching. Both concealed something in their hands. Herr von Spät had meanwhile mounted the bay window steps, set the flask beside him on a small table, and turned around. His white face was like that of a sleeper.

Suddenly a pistol flashed in Trümpelsteg's hand. Hoarse with fear, he screeched in a stuttering voice:

"Stop! Stop! You will kill us all! This is no longer a joke! Stop, or I shoot!"

But Herr von Spät quickly held his index finger over the flask, nicked the fingertip with the knife, and let a drop of blood fall into the flask. At the same instant Trümpelsteg sat thumb-sized in the glass prison.

Frau Cux whimpered in horror, sprang at Herr von Spät, drew a knife, and tried to stab him. Herr von Spät nicked himself anew. And Frau Cux too was stuck, transformed, in the flask.

The company was at first mute with amazement. Then everything broke out in an unwilling uproar. Professor Cux clenched his fists and bellowed like a wounded animal:

"Give me back my wife! Give me back my wife! Or I'll call the police!" But he did not dare approach Herr von Spät.

"Police! Police!" cried the others. "Where is the telephone?"

But Professor Schulze ran from group to group and murmured soothingly in fear:

"For God's sake, ladies and gentlemen, don't provoke him. Just don't provoke him! He is capable of locking us all in the flask. He will also lock up the police. What do we do then? Then we are lost. Calm yourselves, let us think!"

Everything froze in helpless horror. Only a few pulled themselves together and put their heads together. Then they went to Frau Sophie and spoke to her softly and urgently. Sophie nodded to them and walked slowly toward Melchior, who was leaning calmly and cheerfully against the wall. Amid the general motionlessness, the pacing of her small, dainty figure had something strangely solemn about it, heightened still by the glowing nakedness of her skin. She took Melchior by the hand and said pleadingly:

"Let it be enough! Ask Herr von Spät to release the prisoners!" She struggled with rising tears. "Why must I experience all this? Where are you driving me, Melchior? What do you want from me?"

Melchior did not look at her and replied only:

"What do I want from you? Nothing! You decided long ago for those over there. We have nothing more to do with one another!"

Sophie cried out and fell to the floor, wringing her hands.

Then Hans Silferharnisk, the pastor, rose and turned to the desperate ones with a grand gesture:

"Beloved brothers in Christ! This is a judgment of God. We have doubted His omnipotence in our enlightened arrogance. Now He swings His rod of chastisement over us. Let us sink to our knees before the Almighty. Perhaps in His inscrutable goodness He will free us from Satan's snares. Let us pray!"

He knelt down. The others did likewise. But before he could begin his prayer, Herr von Spät took the flask from the table and held it high in the air.

All could see how Trümpelsteg had completely undressed and begun to dance around Frau Cux. He laid one hand protestingly on his breast, the other he held raised as if in oath. Frau Cux seemed to fend him off, but her resistance grew ever weaker, until finally she threw herself around his neck. And the tiny couple sank into passionate lovemaking.

When the pastor saw this, the prayer stuck in his throat and his eyes bulged from their sockets. The others surrounded Herr von Spät and pushed one another aside to see better. Some already began to laugh softly. And after a few moments all broke into irrepressible laughter, fell into one another's arms, kissed, waltzed madly through the room, wept, rejoiced, whimpered with laughter, paused exhausted, looked at the carefree lovers in the flask, and burst out afresh.

Only Professor Cux fell into white-hot rage and tried to hurl himself at Herr von Spät. But the others held him back. Some hurried into the kitchen, returned with a thick rope, and tied him to a heavy armchair so that he could not move a limb.

Herr von Spät, who still held his eyes closed, set the flask on the table and clapped his hands. A light mist formed in the hall. And when it parted, seven white-clad girls stood there and bowed before Herr von Spät.

Deep from the floor came a confused dance music with muffled drum and cymbal beats. Herr von Spät seized one of the girls by the hand and only now opened his eyes, which shone with silvery blue. And when he had opened them, he was sevenfold there and danced with each of the girls at the same time. When the dance was over, he closed them again and was only singly there.

Suddenly, in one of the long walls—which bordered not on another room but formed the outer wall of the house—a great door opened silently that no one had noticed before. One beheld a chamber with set tables. And a voice called out three times loudly:

"To the meal! To the meal! To the meal!"

The voice seemed familiar to Melchior. In the doorway stood the old huckster-woman from the station square, throwing apples among the guests.

All arranged themselves amid laughter and loud talk into couples. Beside each of the men walked a naked woman. Sophie had already crept to Melchior's side. Herr von Spät went with one of the white girls. The remaining six girls brought up the rear. The bound professor was heedlessly forgotten.

The guests took their places. The six girls had seated themselves so that beside each of them a chair remained empty.

The walls of the room were hung with white silk. Two great chandeliers, set with countless candles, radiated a dazzling light. On the table were displayed the rarest delicacies and wines.

The old huckster-woman went from one to another and served the guests amid a stream of inviting phrases. When she poured wine for Melchior, she whispered to him:

"You are clever, my boy, clever. You recognized me at once. But not clever enough. Beware! I wish you well, but must obey to the word!"

"Of whom should I beware?" Melchior whispered back.

"You must know it yourself!" whispered the old woman. "I may say nothing!"

Melchior seized her by the wrist.

"I will not let you go," he hissed passionately. "You must tell me more, must tell me everything!"

The old woman wrested herself free with unexpected strength and murmured as she strode away:

"Ring on finger,
Faces at the window.
Paths cross.
Winds blow southward.
Soon it is time.
They wait. They wait."

"Paths cross? Winds blow southward? Soon it is time?" Melchior repeated inaudibly. He felt a wild, yearning restlessness and felt how weeping constricted his throat. He managed to control himself and look about at the guests. But no one paid him any attention. All were greedily occupied with the food. For a while nothing but rhythmic chewing and slurping was to be heard. Only Sophie had heard the old woman's words and looked at him with large eyes, sad and frightened.

"He will go far away from me," she thought.

The seven girls sat as if sunk in lovely sleep, with downcast eyes. Herr von Spät's eyes too were closed. His head seemed lifeless, as if hewn from stone.

Melchior looked excitedly toward him and thought:

"Why do I hate and love him? He is of my kind and yet my enemy. Why do the boys flee from him? What power does he exercise? What moved him to show his power before these people? Did he do it all for my sake? Did he want to reveal to me who these people are? Did he want to tell me what I already know? I know them. I have long forced them to be naked before me. But I want to go away. What I expected and yet never hoped for has come to pass. Another fellowship calls me. Why do I hesitate? The stranger holds me fast. What does he want from me?"

His gaze fell upon the window. In the window frame he thought he saw Fo's beautiful, defiant wanderer's face, with eyes fixed unwaveringly upon him. But when he looked more attentively, it had vanished.

The guests were still chewing. Then Herr von Spät opened his eyes. And immediately he had multiplied sevenfold and sat beside each of the white girls at the same time. The guests looked up only fleetingly from their plates; they were no longer excessively astonished.

Gradually the meal drew to its close. A general conversation arose and grew more and more animated. Professor Karl Schulze pushed back his chair noisily, struck his glass, and began to speak:

"Most honored guests! Even the seemingly most miraculous becomes natural once one has grown accustomed to it. Nature is nothing other than the sum of the things we are used to. Today we were shaken for a moment by the unfamiliar and almost wanted to take it for a miracle. And now, after scarcely an hour, we sit calmly in a room that has, so to speak, risen from the ground in a few minutes, refresh ourselves with pleasure on foods and drinks that must seem to us conjured from thin air, are no longer especially amazed when one of our circle multiplies sevenfold by merely opening his eyes, forget that two of us, imprisoned in a tiny flask, abandon themselves before everyone's eyes to the boldest delights of love. We have grown accustomed to all this. All these things have become nature for us. What follows from this, ladies and gentlemen? There is nothing supernatural! There are no miracles! There are only facts. And facts are always reasonable. No one can make fools of us, ladies and gentlemen, not us. We incorporate it into our worldview. We remain the same! We are we! Let us raise our glasses and..."

A terrible scream interrupted him. The seven figures of Herr von Spät closed their eyes with a groan. The white girls dissolved into mist. Herr von Spät lay in his ordinary form unconscious on the floor. The walls shifted, turned, grew, shrank together: and before they knew it, all found themselves back in Melchior's salon.

But in the bay window stood Fo and laughed. Herr von Spät writhed in convulsions. His blue eyes stared blindly upward. His whole body twitched as if from unbearable pain.

"Do you feel it now? Do you feel it now?" cried Fo shrilly. "It was beyond your strength. You wanted to rest and play for a moment, eh? Your will was not awake for a moment, eh? Do you feel it now, that you must never sleep? Do you feel it now, that *we* are the masters?"

And he danced around him with great leaps. His body glowed. His hair was dark flames. Faster and faster he circled him with ringing cries.

Melchior looked at the contorted, noble face of the one lying on the floor. Horror and love overwhelmed him. Almost unconscious, he tried to hurl himself at Fo to stop him. But the latter whirled blazing toward the window.

"Take him, Melchior," he cried exuberantly. "We are in your debt. We give him to you. Let him be yours!"

And he laughed once more unbridledly, looked Melchior firmly in the eye, said softly and urgently: "Melchior, we are waiting for you!" and shot away.

### CHAPTER SIX: BEFORE THE DECISION

The convulsions subsided. Herr von Spät began to breathe more calmly. He seemed to sleep. The shimmering blue mist-garment had parted. He lay naked on the floor.

Melchior beheld a body of splendid symmetry. The legs, long and fine-limbed, bore a slender, supple torso. Everything about him was austere and outstretched and yet of the most delicate softness.

Only for a second did Melchior let his eyes rest upon this imperious figure. Before the others could approach, he tore the cloth from the table and threw it over the sleeper. Then he lifted him up, carried him—without concerning himself with the distraught guests—into his study, and bedded him carefully upon the divan. He drew an armchair to the head of the divan, sat down, and contemplated shyly the one lying there motionless.

Sleep had loosened his face, which was constantly trembling with tensions, and lent it an almost serene calm. Only now did Melchior recognize his true features, which the ever-changing expression had hidden from him: it was the countenance of a beautiful god-image, barely perceptibly distorted toward the elongated, indeed the sickly.

But after only a few minutes the features tensed again. A trembling passed through the body. The sleeper made an inhuman effort and tore his eyes open suddenly; they were almost colorless and seemed to see nothing. Only after some time did he sit up, perceive Melchior, stare at him, and let himself fall back into the cushions. So he remained for a long while. Then he said hoarsely and haltingly:

"I came too late. I had warned you too late. The boy Fo is free. They consider me their worst enemy. I had come to your house to take the ring from you. Then sleep overcame me. Why did you spare me?"

"The sleeper was not my enemy," answered Melchior. "I recognized that you are my brother."

Herr von Spät started up and cried vehemently:

"I will never sleep again!"

"Never sleep again?" asked Melchior, taken aback. "How am I to understand that! You can only mean that figuratively?"

"I will never sleep again," replied Herr von Spät, and his eyes widened and grew darker. "When I sleep, the enemies tear me apart. Sleep always lies in wait. I was playing. Then it overwhelmed me for the last time. But I am its master. Our body is not earth. Our body is music, is counter-play of the starry world."

Melchior lowered his head and said softly: "I love the earth. I do not want to be master, I want to give myself away!"

Herr von Spät made an impatient gesture.

"You speak like the boys!" he said angrily.

"Who are the boys?" asked Melchior quickly. "Who is Fo?"

Herr von Spät hesitated. Almost reluctantly he answered at last:

"No one knows, no one knows their true form. As wandering boys they approach you, as fleeting girls, as scurrying animals. Thus they lure you into chaos and darkness. Somewhere they have a kingdom to which I cannot find the entrance. But they are never there. They are always here. Perhaps they are there and here at the same time. Everywhere they carry on their doings and carry you off in reeling dances. I must track down the way to them. I must destroy their kingdom. The unbridled free ones must be in my service. All must become mine, otherwise I am not secure in those I have already subjugated: Fo has escaped, the freest, strongest, boldest of them. No darkness may remain around them, no night, no refuge. No more change may they know, no transformation from form to form. Everything must become bright around them. Their indiscriminate wild love must die. They shall be driven from the wellspring of sleep. No one shall be allowed to sleep anymore!"

Herr von Spät had risen. His body seemed transparent. One could perceive only the shimmering outlines.

When he turned his face upward, the ceiling of the room had vanished. And from the darkness there inclined toward him a countenance that was like a mirror-image of his own, dimly luminous.

"Who are you? Who are you?" cried Melchior, trembling.

Herr von Spät's form grew to immeasurable height and became ever more misty. Icy coldness made Melchior's blood stand still. But he was unable to turn his gaze away.

"Choose, Melchior," called Herr von Spät, and his voice sounded like the distant ringing of glass bells. "If you want to go to the boys, you need only call, and they will carry you off into sweet night; you will become one of theirs and forget everything that you were and are. If you want to come to us, then knock on the wall of this room—and a gate will open for you, a way will open for you to mastery in light. Consider it well! The way to us is full of dangers. You stride through all the horrors of the world. You are still free. When you have chosen, you will have decided. A return then means destruction. We will not spare you."

Herr von Spät's form had completely dissipated during these words. The ceiling closed. The lamps burned dimly. The divan was empty.

Melchior found himself alone in the room.

## PART TWO: THE WANDERING

### CHAPTER SEVEN: THE OPEN GATE

Melchior had buried his head in his hands and was brooding.

"How does this stranger know what I dream of?" he whispered with dry lips. "Is he at home in that heavenly world that swirls around us in great arcs and scatters into the endless? Is he master in brighter, lighter light?"

He sprang up and paced restlessly back and forth in the room. His steps became faster, more violent. His heart pounded like a hammer. It seemed to him as if a bell in him were ringing shrilly to the storm.

"I must have certainty!" he suddenly cried out and struck the room's wall with his fists.

A rushing arose as if all the tones of a harp were sounding together in confusion. Swaying columns of cool fragrance rose slowly upward. A wide gate had opened. Outside a sea swelled in green-blue night and breathed calmly. A great white bird with shimmering drooping feathers spread enormous wings.

And Melchior saw how from afar a narrow sailing ship approached the shore soundlessly. The many-voiced rushing in the air broke off abruptly. Evil dead silence entered.

A slight horror paralyzed him. He could not move a limb. Sweeter and more agonizing became his rigidity, more unbearable his hesitation. Then the small clock in his room raised itself to a bright, silvery stroke. The paralysis receded. Tears dripped from his eyes. With arms spread wide he strode through the gate and stepped out into the night.

After only a few steps he heard loud, breathless calling. He thought he heard the voice of his wife, of Trümpelsteg, of Professor Cux, and of many others. From all sides dark figures stormed toward him. "Seize him, seize him!" cried a muffled, embittered voice. Melchior felt himself grasped from behind. An iron hand reached for his throat and throttled him. A black cloth was thrown over his head. He lost consciousness and sank to the ground.

The first thing he felt, awakening from his unconsciousness, was a slow swaying. He also heard distinctly the rushing of waves striking the hull. He raised his head and looked about him. All around stretched shoreless nocturnal sea. He lay bound on the deck of a small ship. Beside him crouched motionless a muffled figure.

The whole ship was full of muffled ones who staggered about mutely and gesticulating vividly. The storm whistled, the sea ran high. Past the greenish moon, which cast a blind strip of light on the helplessly reeling waves, tattered ice-gray clouds chased by.

Hours passed. No one spoke a word. Melchior lay there exhausted and gazed into the howling darkness.

At the ship's prow a movement arose. Shadowy figures struggled to light a torch. Sparks sprayed up and died. A flame licked upward, ducked down, was caught by the wind and scattered. New sparks sprayed. Again a flame crackled. Black clouds of smoke blew churning upward. The torch burned.

A giant of a man stood at the outermost forward end of the ship and swung the torch over his head. Faster and faster became his movements, so that in the air a racing, fiery circle formed around him. The others crowded together and peered tensely into the distance.

Melchior perceived it all with anxious expectation. From the torch small, reddish flames detached themselves, flickered wildly about and dissolved.

Then at the farthest horizon a small flame likewise flared up, grew trembling, lifted itself from the ground and drew the same racing circle as the torch on the ship, which now broke into twitching movements and wrote a long series of luminous signs into the night. The torch from the shore answered.

To Melchior it seemed the first greeting of a new world. He drank in the strange flame-writing of the unknown land, his heart gave to each sign from over there a flaming answer, it seemed to him as if the muffled torch-swinger were sending this answer of his heart to the ever more closely surging shore.

The sails were furled. The anchor rattled down to the bottom. A long while of waiting passed. Finally through the rushing of the waves one heard long-drawn calls. The man at the ship's prow shouted back at them.

After some time a small boat pulled alongside the ship, and several figures, muffled like the ship's crew, clambered aboard with smoldering oil lanterns in their hands.

The crew stepped aside, made way for them, and pointed wordlessly at Melchior.

The newcomers approached him with creaking steps, threw a black cloth over him again, and tied it together over him with ropes. He felt how they lifted him and lowered him into the small boat. He wanted to scream and could not. His breath failed him, and he sank once more into unconsciousness.

He only came to again when he felt his own feet walking. The bonds had been removed from him. But he could see nothing. Darkness shrouded everything. Before him, behind him, on both sides he heard the shuffling creeping of many feet. Sometimes the breath of a breath brushed him.

So it went lightlessly through endless corridors. Sometimes doors seemed to open. Then chambers were crossed and the steps sounded more echoing, the breathing more audible.

Melchior thought of nothing at all. Everything in him was extinguished. He wondered that his foot found solid ground. He believed he was wandering through empty air.

All at once the procession halted. Someone pounded thunderously on a metal disc. The tones died away. Everything remained soundless and dark as before.

At this moment life returned to Melchior. He crouched like an animal, to hurl himself with clawed fingers upon his silent guard. But he grasped into empty air. In all directions he struck about him in wild impetuosity. But he hit no one. He was alone.

And the darkness parted suddenly, and a superabundance of light streamed upon him, stabbed into his eyes, made him tremble, stagger, fall, tore him up and felled him again. Once more he pulled himself together, opened his eyes, and took a few steps.

An immense hall, lined with red velvet, surrounded him, along the back wall of which a long table stretched. Behind the table, in red robes, three muffled figures sat enthroned.

But along the walls sat all the men and women he had known in his life, directing sharp glances at him and whispering softly among themselves.

### CHAPTER EIGHT: THE JUDGMENT

"Who dared to bind me?" cried Melchior loudly. "Who dared to drag me here by force?"

The hissing in the hall ceased instantly. The muffled ones fell silent. Melchior clenched his fists.

"I want an answer!" he screamed in a terrible voice. But all remained mute as before.

Melchior could no longer contain himself. He stepped up close to the table behind which the muffled ones sat and raised his fist to strike.

Then he heard a hard, bright voice call:

"You stand before your judges, Melchior!"

The cry struck him like a whiplash. His feet no longer bore him. He swayed. Shattered, he fell to his knees and bowed his head.

"Accusers, step forward!" cried the same bright voice. It seemed to emanate from one of the muffled ones. But they sat there rigid and did not stir.

A whispering, murmuring, throat-clearing arose. The crowd along the walls began to move. One after another stood up, stepped forward, took a place at the long table with their backs to the muffled ones, and looked at Melchior with empty, white eyes.

And Melchior recognized friends and enemies, relatives and neighbors, comrades and servants. All faces were gray and as if covered with dust, the features distorted and hollowed out, the mouths wide and black opened, the lips shimmering bluish.

Melchior searched for his wife. She stood at the very front and smiled at him madly and covetously. He scarcely recognized her; she had become even smaller and crouched as if under a heavy burden. Not far from her stood Professor Cux with disheveled gray-speckled red beard. Beside him bulged Trümpelsteg's spongy figure; his long ape-arms dangled incessantly back and forth. Frau Cux leaned against him, gaunt and pointed, had raised her skirt with one hand and showed a thin, stick-like leg.

"You never wore the slippers," croaked Frau Sophie in a hoarse voice, "that I painstakingly embroidered for you for a whole year: You did not love me!"

"You were never interested in my chemical discoveries," lisped Professor Cux threateningly. "You were always only occupied with your own. I was indifferent to you."

"You spoke all my thoughts before I had even properly thought them myself," Trümpelsteg nasaled. "You hollowed me out and made me empty, so that I had nothing more to say. You had no pity for me!"

"Oh my calves, my beautiful calves!" whimpered Frau Cux. "You never admired them, so they have become sticks. You were heartless to me!"

And after every accusation the remaining accusers raised their arms and screeched shrilly.

New figures gathered before Melchior. As if in fog he saw the narrow, suffering face of his mother, the furrowed features of his father. They sank wordlessly back into the crowd. The crooked fingers and the tall, threatening form of a hunchbacked great-aunt lit up; she complained tearfully:

"You always laughed when I wanted to read to you the beautiful verses from my album that my childhood friend had written in it. I showed them to no one but you, and you laughed. So everything I held dear has died with me."

Forgotten schoolfriends stuck out their tongues at him, made faces at him, showed him fists.

One among them, a slight, gentle boy with white, delicate forehead and long eyelashes, stood apart and looked shyly toward Melchior. When Melchior beheld him, a choking rose in his throat; he stretched out his hand.

But already another playmate stood there, with bold brown Roman face, pressing his lips together bitterly.

A snow-white girl with long-fingered, weary hands and amber-yellow eyes brushed her forehead as if she had forgotten something and nodded at him mechanically like an automaton doll.

"You here too?" cried Melchior and wanted to approach her. But ever new throngs, of whom he could distinguish only a few faces, pressed forward in the brownish haze. All murmured incessantly to themselves. But Melchior could understand no word.

And the old huckster-woman ran busily back and forth before the crowd, cast angry glances at Melchior, and babbled breathlessly:

"He was always going away, always going away. I sat at the station. I saw it. I know. I know."

The murmuring of the accusers grew louder and passed into a barking laughter that seemed to have no end.

Then the voice of the judge resounded:

"You have heard the charges. Do you confess yourself guilty?"

Melchior let his head sink and said softly:

"I am guilty. Every step I took is forfeited in guilt. We kill when we live. But who will presume to be judge?"

In the hall it became still.

"Then you are guilty of death!" cried the voice of the judge. And the three muffled ones rose from their thrones.

"Here is no one who can judge me," answered Melchior calmly, and rose from his kneeling position. "I know no judge above me but myself. Who accuses me? I heard nothing but mad cursing and murmuring of substanceless shadows!"

At his words the accusers broke into a whimpering and surged around him like hissing waves. But the muffled ones paid heed neither to him nor to the accusers and repeated together the sentence of the invisible judge:

"You are guilty of death!"

They beckoned to two figures carved from wood at the entrance of the hall. These approached Melchior, seized him by the hands, and led him away.

And again it went through dark corridors, up stairs, down stairs, until they halted before a small, sparsely lit door; they unlocked it, pushed Melchior inside, and locked it behind him.

Melchior stood in a narrow, gleaming chamber. One wall was entirely of glass, through which the hot blue of the sky penetrated. He perceived nothing but this flooding blue, which reflected itself insubstantially in the bright walls.

From the floor and the walls streamed a suffocating heat that took Melchior's breath away. It occurred to him that he had dreamed of this room as a child, when the whispering shadows came with enormous scissors to cut off his fingers.

Fear chased him up and down. "Flee!" he thought. "Only flee!" And he rushed toward the small door. But scarcely had he grasped the door handle than it transformed in his hands into red-glowing iron, so that he let go of it with a scream. At the same time the walls grew higher and higher: instead of before the small door he stood before an enormous red-glowing gate.

With his last strength he staggered to the window to open it. But already iron bars shot forth from all sides and the window was densely barred.

Then the gate screeched on its hinges. The two wooden men entered, carrying a black, blood-stained cloak before them. They stopped before Melchior, bowed, and spoke in a toneless voice:

"We have come to lay the black cloak upon you."

Melchior answered nothing. And they laid the cloak over his shoulders, drew forth hammers and nails, and nailed it fast to Melchior's shoulders. The nails penetrated deep into his flesh. Blood streamed down him and defiled him.

The men led him down a spiral staircase. Through a low portal they stepped outside onto a square surrounded on three sides by houses, by the sea. In the middle of the square a scaffold had been erected, beside which, leaning on a broad sword, a muffled figure stood.

Melchior's gaze fell upon the houses that surrounded the square. He knew them all; they were the houses in which he had once dwelt. Gathered together from all corners of the world, they united here around the blood-scaffold: peasant huts, palaces, gabled houses, hotels, tenement barracks, villas, and skyscrapers. Everywhere the windows behind which he had lived were boarded up.

A dense crowd of people pushed back and forth on the square. And again Melchior recognized the misty shadows of his accusers, who held one another by the hands and performed a howling round-dance around the scaffold.

A trumpet blast shook the air. Countless small, hoarse bells began to ring in terrible haste. The accusers crouched down on the ground and waited tensely. Melchior was led up onto the scaffold by the wooden men. The executioner followed him.

Then a great bird with white shimmering, long drooping plumage flew over the crowd. All stared up at the bird. In Melchior's breast a ringing began. He felt how all his blood streamed to his heart. Laughing brightly he snatched the sword from the executioner with lightning speed, swung it wide, and struck his head from his body.

A gust of wind swept across the square. Melchior saw how from the sea a gigantic wave arose and rolled rapidly toward the city. But ahead of the wave a horse with streaming mane raced and stopped before him. He swung himself, still laughing, into the saddle. The horse stormed away with the speed of the wind.

Behind him a thousand-voiced, gurgling scream shrilled. He looked around. The wave had swallowed everything.

### CHAPTER NINE: THE CALL

With the anguished cries of the drowning in his ears, Melchior rode on senseless. At the foot of a densely wooded mountain he halted and dismounted. He knelt at a brook hastening valleyward, scooped with his hand from the ice-cold water, and drank.

Scarcely had he drunk than it became calm in him. The accusers had detached themselves from him. They had sunk away somewhere; they had no more part in him.

He rose to ride on. But the horse had vanished. Only the white bird circled above him in the evening light and flew calling toward the forest. He followed it and was soon enveloped by green twilight.

A narrow path wound steeply upward between tall beeches. Melchior felt himself seized by a slight dizziness; he looked behind him and perceived that at his back an abyss yawned: with every step he took, the portion of the forest he had traversed sank into empty space; the abyss ran close behind him.

The air became thinner and colder. Ever more lightly he climbed upward. At last the tree trunks thinned out. And when the forest behind him had dissolved into nothing, he found himself on a bare high plateau. Cold night drew up.

Then Melchior heard a barking. A great wolf gleamed at him with green eyes. From all sides long-drawn answering howls resounded. Out of the darkness hasty shadows shot forth. In the starlight white fangs gaped and shimmered. Hundreds of wolves stormed up panting.

Melchior uttered a scream and ran forward in great leaps. But the wolves overtook him and drew a chasing ring around him that grew tighter and tighter.

In helpless fear he stopped. Then the wolves also halted and settled themselves around him in a wide circle. He dared not stir. As soon as he made the slightest movement, they began to stir and growl evilly.

So he stood there rigid. Whether it was hours or only minutes he did not know. His eyes wide open, he looked toward the horizon, which slowly grew brighter. The sun rose. Tears came to his eyes. He looked into the ever whiter light, stretched out his arms toward it, and stepped on without being aware of it.

The wolves swelled cloudily, became transparent, filled themselves with the white fire of the sun, and scattered luminously in all directions with piercing howls.

Toward noon the view veiled itself with smoldering haze. From far away a wind wafted over the foul stench.

With burning eyes Melchior dragged himself onward, painfully longing for coolness, until he came to a high board fence. The gate was open. He entered and found himself in a grass-overgrown courtyard. In the middle of the courtyard stood a dilapidated stone hut with empty window holes, behind which tattered red curtains fluttered.

The whole space swarmed with gaunt people with tall fur caps over small bird-of-prey faces. They stood behind wooden sales stands and, shouting over one another, offered large yellow mushrooms with light-green spots for sale. From the mushrooms clouds of yellow vapor rose up. The sun burned frighteningly red behind steaming veils.

"Buy, buy mushrooms!" the bird-of-prey faces called hoarsely among themselves. "Buy mushrooms! These are the last! The earth is evaporating! The sun is rotting! Buy mushrooms while supplies last! The forest is dying! The world is bursting! Clearance sale! Clearance sale!"

Melchior reeled. Every breath with which he drew in the fermenting stench let creeping heat trickle into his veins. His limbs grew heavier. The slack air stroked gropingly down him. He saw the faces of the screaming ones only as shapeless shadows now. The wounds on his shoulders, around which the nailed-fast black cloak hung, hurt sharply and sweetly and sent slumbering shivers over his back.

The voices of the merchants passed into a frenzied twittering. The mushrooms on their tables swelled up, burst into new mushrooms, formed a surge of swelling limbs. And above all the hanging clouds circled choking, formed themselves into drifting shapes, dissolved, rose higher, sank, pressed down by the slow hot wind, to the ground and mingled with the softened earth into seething mud.

An unbridled laughter arose in the background. Out of the surging tangle the old huckster-woman emerged and danced around Melchior in obscene leaps. She was stark naked. Her gray hair hung in strands down her back. Her withered, sagging breasts slapped about her decaying body with every leap. She stretched her arms high and screamed, possessed, in chorus with the merchants:

"Buy, buy mushrooms! These are the last! Buy while supplies last! The earth is evaporating! The sun is rotting! The forest is dying! The world is bursting! Clearance sale! Clearance sale!"

With clawed fingers she reached into the drifting vapor and suddenly held a young woman by the hand, who stood there languidly and swayed idly in her hips. Her skin was yellow. Her eyes blinked greenishly. Her protruding lips and the broad buds of her breasts burned in violet red.

The old woman slapped her resoundingly on thighs and breasts and shrieked along with it:

"Clearance sale! Clearance sale!"

And the cries of the merchants shrilled and the swarming mushrooms fell from the tables, sank into the muddy earth, and grew up from it as a forest of fleshy trees.

Melchior's breath stopped. He hissed like a cat.

"A way out! A way out!" it screamed within him. It occurred to him that he carried a large pocketknife with him. He drew it out, opened it, and stabbed, red gleam around him, at the huckster-woman and the young woman. He saw their blood flow. But it transformed into reddish vapor. The deep wounds closed up again immediately. And the two women struck up a fat, cooing laughter.

The old woman grasped at him with twining arms. Already she held him embraced:

Then he closed his eyes and saw in a sudden flash of blue light that broke forth from his interior, against a cold sky of circling stars, an enormous body forming itself.

He tore himself from the throttling embrace, drew a deep breath, and began to sing with a high, far-echoing voice.

And from all sides suns closed multicolored arcs around him. Resounding echo took up his singing, dissolved it into a thousand-voiced whirl, and fell silent in long reverberation.

The lights went out. It was day again. Far stretched green glaciers, from which in the distance a crystalline edifice rose. Ulrich von Spät stood before Melchior, bowed to him, and spoke:

"You have found the way. Now you are ours. You have dissolved the judgment of men, tamed the greed of animals, banished the revenge of the rotting earth. Servant only to the stars, you are master over men, animals, and earth. Enter and let yourself be crowned our brother."

Melchior felt how a dead coldness spread in him. But Ulrich took him by the hand and led him.

"Night and chaos have sunk away," said Ulrich. "Sleep has no power here. Day and light endure for you always, even when you descend into the world of sleepers to work among them unrecognized."

Ever closer gleamed the crystalline castle. They strode over broad steps, through gates, over new steps, through new gates. A hall opened, whose walls played palely in all colors of the rainbow. Round columns bore a high, transparent dome. Melchior froze.

"Wait here until you are called; on the table there lies your staff!" said Ulrich and left him.

Melchior leaned against a column, then recognized it was hewn from ice. The room was empty. Only on one wall stood a large table, layered from green blocks of ice. But on the table lay an ivory staff, adorned with pearls that united into script-characters.

When Melchior took it in his hand, all his clothes fell from him. The nail-marks on his shoulders closed and healed. He stood there naked in pale rays. He no longer felt the frost. Everything in him was light and empty.

A soft ringing of bells began and gradually grew louder. The door slowly opened. And Melchior looked out onto a wide plaza filled with gleaming figures. Their bodies were as if made of glass, their eyes blue stones. Ulrich was among them and smiled. In the middle of the plaza rested a glass shrine adorned with white stones.

The bells boomed deafeningly loud. To Melchior it seemed as if they were ringing directly above his head. Now the ringing seemed to come from all sides: from the empty sky, from the icy cliffs, from the bottomless abysses that surrounded the plaza. The whole air, the crystalline edifice, the glass bodies rang.

Along the sides of the plaza stood in rigid lifelessness a band of boys. They held their heads inclined and looked with lowered lids at the ground.

At a sign from the glass lord, two of them stepped forward with dead movements and went toward the shrine. Carefully they took from it a flashing crown and held it high into the light.

Melchior slowly walked down the broad, flat steps that led to the plaza. The ringing of bells fell silent. He felt himself alone and lost on the enormous staircase. His steps became ever more measured and rigid. His gaze turned to stone.

So he had reached the middle of the plaza. Then the two crown-bearers looked at him; a formless memory seemed to awaken in them.

It shot through Melchior like a blow: he had recognized in the eyes of the two boys the eyes of Fo.

"They belong to Fo's band," it went through his mind with terrible clarity. "They have been struck into chains by the lords, they are frozen, as I am freezing. What did I do? What am I doing? I betray my own, to whom I belong, for whom I have waited longingly all my life, without whom I am abandoned and barren. They came to receive me into their band. And I betray them to the enemy! I shatter life!"

And he looked with horrified eyes over the icily shimmering plaza, looked at the glass lords, looked at the pale figures of the spellbound boys and the weary, tormented faces of the two crown-bearers before him.

"They have forgotten themselves and seek in vain to remember," he thought. And a horror seized him such as he had never felt, when he saw his own strange hands slowly stretching toward the crown.

Trumpets blared silver. The crown-bearers knelt before him. Still his hand had not touched the crown.

Then he heard a soft voice call directly above him: "Do you not want to leave here?"

It was Fo's voice.

The death-rigidity receded from Melchior. "He is here!" he knew at once. For a second he still hesitated and looked at Ulrich's face, which glittered at him threateningly. Then he threw his arms into the air and cried:

"I want to leave!"

At the same instant he felt himself seized by slender arms. Cool lips pressed upon his brow. Gentle wind caught him and bore him wafting away. The glaciers, the crystalline castle, the glass lords sank into blue mist. The wind rushed faster and warmer. A light descending. He opened his eyes.

Far stretched moonlit meadow. Countless fireflies danced through sweet summer air. Torches burned. From everywhere voices sounded. He saw Fo's face bent over him, smiled at him quietly, and fell into dark sleep.

### CHAPTER TEN: THE CRUCIFIXION AND THE ANIMALS

Moonlight shone harder and brighter. The torches had burned out.

In flying leaps, silver figures danced across the meadow. Like fluttering light, their hair flew around their brows.

One stood motionless in the middle of the ring with raised arms. Melchior recognized Fo's slender figure.

Then, with singing cries, a dancer reached into the trembling moonbeams, tensed his arm and held a shimmering spear in his hand. Stepping back with one foot, leaning far back, he hurled the spear at Fo and struck him in the heart.

Fo swayed and tore the spear from his breast. From his wound shot a pale stream, ran down to earth, swelled and serpentined as a hurrying brook through the meadows.

A cry from the boys. A hasty pressing. And all knelt down by the brook, bent over it and drank.

While the white blood flowed from him ever more fiercely and gushing, Fo grew ever smaller, thinner, more slender, until he collapsed staggering, and his whole body transformed into sounding waves.

The brook dried up. The boys sank back wearily, stretched out idly in the soft grass and fell asleep with open eyes.

A trembling ran through them. From their foreheads emerged shimmering mists that formed into spheres that circled each other and rose higher and higher. Intertwining hundredfold through one another, they united into a great ball that swung in rushing whirl around the moon, drew ever narrower circles around it and melted merging into it. The moon swelled monstrously. A soft hesitation. And it roared unbridled in spraying rays down to earth.

But from the rays stepped forth Fo and wafted, drawing light-mist after him, across the meadow. He bent over each of the sleepers and touched them lightly on the shoulders with outstretched pointed fingers. Awakened by his touch, the boys sprang up and burst into a far-echoing laughter.

A sweet intoxication seized Melchior. Streaming with tears, he had to join loudly and uncontrollably in the laughter, until meadow, moonlight and boys blurred into one for him and danced as flying lights before his eyes.

At once he was surrounded by the band.

"He has awakened! He has awakened!" they all called together. "Welcome! Welcome!"

"You were far away, Melchior," said Fo, taking his hand. "We could not find you. You wandered where we could not follow. Clouds bore you away, so that you forgot us. Your heart did not call us, so we had no power to approach you. Into those regions we can only penetrate when we are called. Only when you dwelt among the glass lords and saw our imprisoned ones did you remember us. Your awakened love compelled us here."

"Where I was?" spoke Melchior, searching in his memory. "I know only that they held me captive. Unknown ones wanted to judge me. But I struck my executioner's head off. Then the sea had swallowed everything. I wandered far. The path sank behind me. Wolves fell upon me in frosty night. Dying world drew me into sultry haze. How I escaped has vanished from me, although it was only yesterday..."

"Only yesterday?" cried Fo. "Look here!" And he led him to a small lake that the bushes had hidden until now. Melchior looked at the moonlit surface and perceived his reflection. His hair was white. His skin wrinkled. His eyes sunken. Melchior recoiled.

"Do not fear, Melchior," said Fo. "Now you are among your own. And when you have been crucified, you will be wholly ours."

And Fo took Melchior's face between his cool hands and kissed him gently on the brow. Then Melchior felt how everything he had experienced became strange to him and hovered around him only in indistinct images. Fo kissed him on both eyes—and the images dissolved into sparks. Fo kissed him on the mouth—and everything had sunk deep away.

"Follow me," said Fo, and led him back to the others. First dawn was graying. A warm breath of wind hissed wakingly through the grass.

"Now you will be nailed to the cross," spoke Fo. "Your blood pours out into the world and you grow young from the ground."

And he turned to the boys and called:

"Raise the cross!"

A loud cry of jubilation answered:

"Crucify, crucify him!"

Melchior looked up and saw in the middle of the meadow a mighty cross towering into the brightening sky. In the next instant he was seized by the boys and dragged to the cross. He let everything happen to him. A boy stepped up to him, a faintly glowing crown of thorns in his hand. The crown was woven of light. The thorns glimmered in twitching rays.

"Kneel down, Melchior!" he called in a bright voice.

Melchior obeyed. And the boy pressed the crown of paling moonlight into his hair. The rays scratched his brow. Drops of blood welled forth and ran over his face. But to him it was as if cool flower petals brushed his brow. A light dizziness made him shudder.

Then two boys seized him by the shoulders and carried him, climbing up the two narrow ladders that leaned against both arms of the cross, high aloft. Others climbed after them, sat astride the beams and held Melchior's arms, who stared with wide-torn eyes into the first red of dawn.

The trunk of the cross seemed to him soft as balled mist. The flashing nails that the boys drove through his hands and feet felt like cold shadows that penetrated sharp into his flesh. His whole body seemed to him a light shadow.

So he hung, a shadow on the shadow of a cross, high between heaven and earth, his face turned toward the slowly rising sun. But he saw nothing more. Heaven and earth sank into one for him.

Then the first sunbeam struck his breast and tore his body open. Foaming, his blood gushed forth in a mighty stream.

Having become seeing again, he saw it divide into countless arms, disappear deep into the earth, hurry back and forth through dark tangle of veins, press upward in a thousand places, well forth, squeeze through rocks, spray scattering over cliffs, stream onward in drunken calm and lose itself into endlessness.

He looked upward. And from his eyes rose floating blue sky and vaulted over him. From the earth swelling sap rose up in him.

No longer did he hang on a cross. He himself was earth and cross. Roots penetrated deep into nocturnal ground and sucked in moist fire.

His outstretched arms sprouted into wild branches. His head lifted into the immense. His green hair waved in the wind. Bright springs broke forth beneath his roots.

And as he listened, his hearing became flute tones: Fo sat in his shadow playing on a willow-reed.

The whole band fell into circling whirl at his playing. Bodies stretched, shrank together; limbs extended, curved, grew thicker, narrower, blunter, heavier and lighter; new faces threw off the old ones like masks. A twitching, wriggling, tugging, writhing...

And already some boys flew up as great birds, whirred in the sunlight and nested in Melchior's hair. Lions caressed his trunk gently and at peace. Snakes coiled around him sparkling. Tigers crouched in the bushes and blinked lazily. Lambs hopped playing around lion and tiger. Deer lay beneath his branches. Horses drank from his springs.

More and more pressed near: leopards, cattle, stags, bears, wolves, foxes. All cried and called, growled and cooed, hissed and roared, howled and bleated. Louder and louder sounded Fo's flute. From all bushes peered listening faces.

Then it broke like a cry from Melchior. And while he cried with red mouth, he had become another. A boy he stood among throngs of boys.

Enveloped by Fo's flute-playing, they sang far across the morning meadows:

> "All animals return home to the Garden of Eden."

As the song faded, Fo set aside his flute and stepped up to Melchior, took his hand and spoke:

"You had a name. Do you still know it?"

Melchior tried to think, but he could not remember. He shook his head.

"Have I slept?" he asked. "Have I only forgotten my dream?"

"We all had other names before we were crucified," answered Fo. "Now we shall receive you into our band and give you a new name. But it is not your true name. That you will only hear when you enter the kingdom."

"Which kingdom?"

"Our kingdom! There we are at home. There we play round the ancient fountains and drink of the holy water. There in black mirrors we see all we have lived. For from the dark surfaces rise thousands of forms that we leave behind when we depart from the kingdom and that we must resume when we begin to wander again."

"And why must we wander?"

Fo's eyes shone. He did not answer but asked:

"Do you not want to be everywhere? To be the wind and the rain, the trees and the grass? Do you not want to be part of the sunset and melt into the moon? Do you not want to be every animal and every human? To speak from every mouth and see from every eye? We escape into and out of every form. Wherever we appear, everything turns into a whirlwind, and nothing is lasting."

"But when do we reach the kingdom?" asked Melchior again.

"Today or tomorrow, or in innumerable years. What does time matter? Suddenly we can stand at the crossroads, and one of the roads leads to the kingdom. Or it stretches out to faraway golden shores beyond great waters. Or we open the door into a strange house—and have arrived. Everywhere we can stand at its borders. But until then we must wander. If we stop, we shall never get there."

"And where are we going now?"

"Onward and onward," said Fo, his eyes shining. "And immediately before us lies a great city. When we leave it, our band will have grown larger. And in that city no one will know anymore... But you must have a name. Who shall give it to you? He from whom you receive your name, he is your companion if the band scatters."

Melchior looked long at Fo and then asked:

"Do you want to come with me?"

"Yes," answered Fo. "We have saved each other. So we will stay together."

He beckoned to the boys, who circled around, and spoke solemnly:

"You shall be called Li!"

"Li! Li! Li!" the boys cried.

And Melchior-Li smiled, for the name sounded to him like laughing light.

## PART THREE: THE DEED

### CHAPTER ELEVEN: STUHLBRESTENBURG

The capital city of Stuhlbrestenburg owed its strange appearance to a whimsical fancy of Walter the Second, that merry king in whose reign the great fire had fallen, which had destroyed almost the entire city. When its reconstruction was undertaken, the old king had hit upon the most curious notion of his life. He thought it exceedingly amusing not to have the massive wall-remnants, which had mostly remained standing up to the first floor, torn down, but rather to have the new houses built upon them, as the ground floor, in an entirely different, airier style of architecture, so that each house would consist distinctly recognizably of an old and a new part; moreover, to make this difference stand out even more for the eye, it was to be forbidden to whitewash the blackish old parts, while the new superstructures were to bear a whitish or rosy coloring.

Despite the resistance of a considerable portion of the inhabitants, Walter the Second, after much back-and-forth, got his way. The ground floors of the city risen from the ruins loomed heavy and dark with small low windows upon the earth as before, but above them rose, bright, dainty and fragile, ornate structures with narrow high windows.

All these houses had extensive cellar rooms, beneath which stretched even deeper vaults and passages connected with each other, partially collapsed, dating from the time when Stuhlbrestenburg was still a fortress.

There were located the hideouts of the light-shy elements of the city. Despite all efforts, it had never yet been possible to track them down. The bolder police squads who, hoping for rich reward, had on occasion penetrated into these vaults through the few entrances known to them, had been unable to find their way, had gotten into walled-up or collapsed passages, and after long wandering had always withdrawn again with nothing accomplished.

As a consequence, the insecurity in Stuhlbrestenburg increased alarmingly. The richer the city became, the farther it expanded, the more densely the mysterious vaults became populated. The criminal existences of the entire country seemed magnetically attracted by them and found in the capital their plentiful livelihood.

It was suspected that the poorer classes of the population, who everywhere inhabited the old ground floors where, on account of their uninhabitable darkness, no one else would dwell, stood in secret connection with the criminals and rendered them assistance. But nothing of the sort had ever been able to be proven. Striking was only that these classes in Stuhlbrestenburg made a more sullen, more rebellious, more self-assured impression than anywhere else.

But that perhaps hung together with the whole character of the city, which clearly differed from that of the other cities of the land. Stuhlbrestenburg lay in a wide damp plain under an almost always overcast sky. The air in this region had something strangely exciting and at the same time depressing. The people consumed themselves quickly and lived fiercely and greedily. Poor and rich were industrious and dissolute. In no other city were there so many factories, churches and places of amusement of every kind. Everywhere it looked sultry and somehow unclean. In the dwellings one had the feeling as if the new walls sucked ever anew poisonous moisture from the old foundations.

For several days things had been restless in Stuhlbrestenburg. The police had succeeded in getting hold of one of the criminals dwelling in the underground hideouts. This one had been willing, for high reward and assurance of complete immunity from punishment, to betray all entrances to the vaults known to him, but refused to render guide services to the police squads down below, since he feared for his life if his former comrades should see him. He also warned the officials against venturing down; they had secured themselves there against all surprises.

The authorities deliberated long on what was to be done, for they wanted to put an end once and for all to these insufferable conditions, which made the doings and dealings of the citizens more and more a risky adventure, disturbed all their habits and seriously threatened the whole fabric of their communal life.

After extensive consultations it was decided to occupy the entrances and starve out the criminals. For among the poorer population things had begun to ferment ever more alarmingly. More than ever the authorities were convinced that this class was making common cause with the criminals, especially since some of the betrayed entrances actually were located in old ground-floor dwellings. The occupants of the dwellings in question had been arrested and an otherwise unsuccessful house search conducted, but they had to be released again for lack of evidence, whereby the defiance of the rest assumed even more disquieting forms. All these unpleasant phenomena they believed they could put an end to at one stroke through the starving-out of the "Subterraneans."

But precisely in these days a series of incidents was reported from the neighboring district town of Rattenhusen which, magnified by rumor, put the Stuhlbrestenburg citizens into an even greater agitation and almost made them forget their own troublesome affairs.

It had begun when the religion teacher Flamm of the Rattenhusen gymnasium, a small, stooped man with bitter features who was hated by the students for his fanaticism and his strictness, suddenly in the middle of a lesson interrupted his lecture, stared horrified at a student, jumped up abruptly, addressed the student by a strange name, sank to his knees before him, kissed his hands and begged him lamenting for forgiveness. The surprised students fetched the director, and Flamm was brought to the teachers' room, where he only came to his senses some hours later.

After at first refusing all explanations, he finally told the following:

It was more than twenty years ago that Flamm had had a student of slender figure with a proud, narrow face and long, curly hair. This student aroused his hatred. He was very gifted but of insubordinate disposition and continually displayed a superior cheerfulness. Flamm tried in every way to humiliate him, but it was in vain. He began to observe him outside of school as well and soon established that he sometimes met in nearby villages with strange, oddly dressed boys. After such meetings he always behaved in school in a particularly challenging manner. Flamm then went to the student's father, reported to him what he had observed, warned him of the bad company which apparently encouraged his son in his disobedience, and proposed to take the boy into his own boarding to withdraw him, through stricter supervision, from those evil influences—otherwise he would unfailingly go astray and never become a useful member of human society. The father, after some deliberation, agreed. But when the boy was to move to the teacher's, he had disappeared and had left behind a letter from whose dark hints one could gather that he intended to depart from life. All inquiries remained fruitless.

And now, Flamm continued, a few hours ago he had observed to his horror how the face of the student Ranke, who sat in the first row, gradually transformed into the face of that student who had been missing for twenty years. At first it was only the eyes. Ranke's eyes were gentle and brown, but suddenly he had had the staring gray eyes of the vanished one, and in a few minutes had completely taken on his form.

"I was so frightened," Flamm continued sobbing, "that I could not stir a limb. After twenty years, just think, after twenty years! Such treachery! Such malice! And yet he was dead! Nevertheless he sat there and looked at me, motionless and unceasing, as if I were guilty of his death. I do not know anymore what happened to me. It tore me upward and I had to fall down before him and beg him for forgiveness. He would have murdered me otherwise. I could not endure his gaze."

Some older colleagues of Flamm's did actually remember that mysterious disappearance. But all were deeply astonished that Flamm, who in their opinion had acted completely rightly at that time, now, after twenty years, was losing his mind over it. For they took his statements for the fixed ideas of a madman.

Someone was sent to the dwelling of the student Ranke. They wanted to bring him before Flamm's eyes so that he might possibly convince himself of the untenability of his delusion. But the school servant returned without Ranke and reported that he lay sick in bed and had not been at school at all today.

The astonishment of the teachers reached its peak. It was absurd; they themselves had seen Ranke in the class and spoken with him, all his classmates had been together with him. They inquired at the students' dwellings, but learned that most of them had strangely not come home after school; the rest confirmed that Ranke had attended the lessons. The director now visited Ranke personally and found him lying in high fever. His parents assured him he had been sick since last evening. The director was at a loss.

The next morning more than half the class did not appear for lessons. Immediate inquiries revealed that these students had also not been home overnight. The police were alerted. Throughout the whole land there was searching. But it was in vain. No one had seen them. Nowhere did one learn anything about their whereabouts.

A day later there was talk of an even more unheard-of occurrence. The well-known director of the Rattenhusen Discount Society, Clemens Rotbuch, a somewhat effeminate-looking eccentric with quirky aesthetic and theosophical leanings, had betaken himself to the bank at midday when the personnel was absent, and from a window on the first floor had, with an obliging smile, thrown all the money in the bank onto the marketplace, which at that hour was very busy, where the public, snatching at the hundred- and thousand-thaler notes flying about in the air, fell into such an indescribable tumult that a wild brawl ensued. Two dead and several seriously injured were to be mourned.

Clemens Rotbuch was seized and conveyed to the madhouse. After he had come to himself again and could be questioned, he declared that in the forenoon two boys in storm collars and leather caps had come to him and had strictly commanded him to carry out everything he had then actually done. It was now completely incomprehensible to him how he had come to obey them; he had acted as under a compulsion and had no longer been master of himself.

The doctors believed they could gather from all this that in Clemens Rotbuch, who had long been considered severely nerve-suffering, madness had now definitively broken out. But soon news came from all parts of the city: everywhere such boys had appeared and were seducing the inhabitants to the most improbable actions. All seemed to feel themselves completely defenseless against them and to carry out without objection what those demanded.

But not enough with that—on the same day the gates of the prison had been found open. The guards lay bound and gagged in the henhouse, and the prison director squatted on a perch in full uniform, beat with his arms as with wings, and crowed loudly and piercingly. All the prisoners had escaped. It was supposed they had made their way to Stuhlbrestenburg and were hiding there with the "Subterraneans."

"We can explain these events," wrote the *Rattenhusen Herald* in its leading article, "only as a kind of mass psychosis. How else would it be possible for a band of undisciplined half-grown youths, who obviously belong to the proletarian youth poisoned by the reading of Sherlock Holmes, Karl Marx, and Alexandre Dumas, under the mask of mystical emissaries to seduce our citizenry to actions that positively fly in the face of all customs and conventions. But that is the consequence of that haste and craving for novelty which belong to the driving forces of our time. What yesterday still seemed a miracle has today become an everyday occurrence, so that even the most sober people often no longer know how to distinguish the impossible from the possible. In these stormy times, when everything is topsy-turvy, we can therefore only advise our worthy fellow citizens to consider incredible things credible only when they have been officially confirmed. We must relearn daily, but therefore also need a firm hold. And the only resting pole in the flux of appearances remains still the official sanction. *Sigillum signum veri.* To the high authorities we most earnestly recommend searching for the evildoers so that they do not cause yet more mischief, yet more confusion. *Caveant consules!*"

So much for the leading article. In the feuilleton, however, Medical Councillor Professor Doctor Albrecht Hinkeldey expatiated on the nature of mass psychoses, warned against overwork, passionate brooding, unfruitful fantasies, and recommended taking cold foot-baths before going to sleep and rubbing down the whole body with a damp cloth upon rising.

### CHAPTER TWELVE: THE GOAT IN THE CHURCH

The district judge of Stuhlbrestenburg, Knud Ahlbeck, had just finished the exciting reading of these two articles in the *Rattenhusen Herald*, took another sip of coffee and was about to say goodbye to his wife to go to court, when the teacher Flamm was announced, with whom he had been friends for nearly forty years.

He was alarmed at the teacher's appearance. Flamm began weeping immediately upon entering and could not bring out a word. Ahlbeck shook his hand sympathetically and asked him to take a seat. Frau Ahlbeck poured him a cup of coffee and laid a large piece of cake on his plate.

Flamm sat huddled in the broad armchair, chewing and sobbing alternately.

"My head is all confused," he finally began. "I don't know at all where to start. The misfortune! The disgrace! Just think, after twenty years! I have been removed from office, put out on the street! What shall I do?"

"Yes, what actually happened, old friend?" said Ahlbeck. "I can't make sense of the newspapers. There's only overwrought drivel in them. You were there. Tell me! What is it about?"

But Flamm was still unable to say anything coherent and only exclaimed over and over: "The disgrace! The disgrace!"

"So tell me at last!" Ahlbeck urged.

Flamm began weeping again and stammered with difficulty: "I don't know... I still haven't come to myself... Breadless!... Chased away!... No one wants anything to do with me... You are the only one who has received me..."

"But, but," Ahlbeck interrupted him, "it can't be so bad as all that. You have your pension after all. And besides, people take lively interest in your fate, you can imagine that!"

"What good is their interest to me? They think I'm crazy. Everyone to whom something similar has happened in these days is thought crazy. They have dismissed me. I'm considered dangerous here, I, I, who have kept the youth in discipline, humility, and submission my whole life..."

Flamm grew more and more excited and trembled all over with agitation.

"But God will not be mocked," he continued in a scolding voice. "The blinded ones will realize what they have lost in me. It is a hellish plot. Thanks to the softness of my heart and the tenderness of my conscience, I was carried away the day before yesterday to that compromising step. And now dismissed! Behind all this something is hidden, Ahlbeck, I tell you, something is hidden behind it. What it is, I cannot yet survey, but something is hidden behind it. It is not only directed against me, it is directed against you too, it is directed against everyone, it is directed against State, Religion, Morality. It is a conspiracy, a conspiracy! And you must help me uncover it!"

"But dear Flamm, I don't understand how you come to such adventurous fancies," replied Ahlbeck in astonishment. "What connection exists between the strange vision you had and the fantastic assumption of a conspiracy? Since when can conspirators work magic? You are overworked, overstrained. Your nerves have played a trick on you. You will recover. And we'll put the whole matter right again!..."

Flamm sprang up indignantly.

"What?" he cried. "Overstrained? Vision? Nerves? And how do you explain everything else? Hey? The disappearance of the students? Ranke's illness? The affair with Clemens Rotbuch? The open prison doors? The crowing director? I will tell you what it is. It is the beginning of the end, the overthrow, the ruin! And if action isn't taken in time, all is lost!"

Ahlbeck looked up, taken aback, and said:

"Is all that really true then?"

"All of it," replied Flamm eagerly, "all of it! And much more besides! They don't have any overview yet of what has occurred in Rattenhusen for three days. The people are afraid to speak of their experiences, they don't want to make themselves ridiculous. Perhaps they also tell themselves that nothing happened at all, because they have no desire to lose their minds. And yet it is true! As true as I stand here! Ahlbeck, man, brother, the whole is coming apart at the seams, the whole!"

Here Frau Ahlbeck mixed into the conversation.

"You see, Knud," she cried triumphantly, "you see? Didn't I say so at once? But you took all that for superstition and women's gossip. Who turns out to be right? And my dream from the night before last? What do you say to that now?"

"That you should leave me in peace!" Ahlbeck cried furiously. "My head is already buzzing. And then one is supposed to play judge all day, and just today when the great trial takes place... One can't survey all this in the first excitement. One must think it over calmly. And you, Flamm, shouldn't take such foolish words in your mouth as overthrow, end, ruin. We're nowhere near that yet. You allude to the Subterraneans? We'll be able to handle them—and even if the whole rabble should join forces with them. We are the masters! We have created the culture of this land! We will know how to protect it!... Perhaps behind your affair there really is a criminal plot; but perhaps it can also be explained relatively harmlessly... Overthrow? There is no such thing as overthrow! The way people have always lived, that's the way they'll go on living. What sort of insane expressions are those!... Come, calm yourself. I have another hour before I must go to court. We'll sit down in a little wine tavern and discuss everything comfortably together."

They went.

When they passed the half-open portal of the cathedral, they stopped in astonishment. From within sounded music. A choir of many hundreds of voices was singing an exuberantly jubilant song.

"There you have it again!" said Flamm with a sour face. "The new spirit! The church is being made into a theater!"

"How beautifully they sing," Ahlbeck interrupted him, for he was a great lover of music. "I have never heard such beautiful singing. Let's listen for a bit!"

When they entered the church, they noticed that it was full of people. Apparently the singing had lured in many passers-by. Neither Ahlbeck nor Flamm knew on what occasion this morning concert was taking place.

Then Ahlbeck perceived the chief public prosecutor, Johannsen, standing before him. He touched him on the shoulder. Johannsen turned around and whispered in astonishment:

"What, you here too? What's going on here? I hadn't heard that a church celebration was announced for today. I heard singing outside, entered, and..."

"Then you're in the same case as I am; what can this mean?" Ahlbeck gave back just as softly.

"Just listen," said Johannsen, "what sort of music that is! Those aren't church songs, are they! A bit worldly, eh? It seems nevertheless that a service is to be held: the altar candles are burning."

The song that resounded through the church from the organ choir went over into an ever more fiery tempo, rose rebelliously and excitingly to raging lust and issued into a passionate dance tune.

Upon the listeners lay something like a spell. They seemed to have forgotten where they were. Their faces glowed as in fever. They breathed heavily. Some from time to time uttered groaning sounds that rang like a suppressed lustful laughter. Occasionally a jerk went through their bodies. Their limbs began to twitch rhythmically.

The singing became ever more monstrous. Already drum beats mingled with the tones. Raging violin passages hissed forth. Trumpet blasts drove to ever more breathless frenzy. And when the organ joined in with subterranean thunder, the people restrained themselves no longer.

Senseless, they sank into each other's arms and began stamping and screaming to dance. Flamm, Ahlbeck, and Johannsen hopped like goats and linked a clumsy ring-dance with some market women. Ahlbeck bellowed continuously a booming:

"Ho-hop! Ho-hop!"

Johannsen called in between with a commanding voice:

"Changez les dames! Changez les dames!"

And Flamm whimpered amid whining laughter:

"The ruin! The downfall! Hurrah! Hurrah! Hurrah! For Thine is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever Amen." And he sprang with his thin legs higher and higher.

With a mighty Hallelujah the music closed.

At this moment the aged Consistorial Councillor Pistorius was seen mounting the pulpit in full vestments. All fell suddenly silent. Then Flamm seized the district judge convulsively by the arm and croaked:

"There they are again! There they are again!"

"Who? What?" asked the judge and looked around in all directions.

"The eyes! The student's eyes! The Consistorial Councillor! He is here! He's coming after me! For God's sake, what shall I do? Where shall I hide? Help! Mercy! Mercy!"

He sank to his knees and whimpered. Those standing around fell into confusion. One after another knelt down fearfully. The excitement communicated itself ever further. And in a few minutes all lay on their knees and groaned with wrung hands:

"Mercy! Mercy!"

From the pulpit came a loud, never-ending laughter. The full red face of the Consistorial Councillor became narrower and whiter. His small black little eyes had widened and acquired a gray, staring gleam. For a moment he looked like a half-grown boy. But this sight too transformed, and on the pulpit stood, its shaggy forelegs propped on the reading desk, a snow-white he-goat and bleated brightly and piercingly.

The listeners did not know what to do. Cold sweat broke out on them. They beat their foreheads on the floor and groaned their:

"Mercy! Mercy!"

Johannsen babbled with white lips over and over:

"Quousque tandem Catilina? Quousque tandem Catilina!"

Ahlbeck's head turned red. He stretched out his arms and thundered over the heads of the others:

"Hold out! Hold out!"

But no one listened to him. Sharper, more shrill, more imploring rang their cry for mercy. Only Flamm stood there, distorted with rage, with clenched fists. He had found himself again. Cuttingly his voice rose above the wailing:

"Thus speaks the Prophet: Woe to the abominable, filthy, tyrannical city. She will not obey, nor let herself be chastened. How desolate she has become, that beasts dwell therein! And whoever passes by whistles at her and claps his hand over her!"

Thus he ranted on for minutes and stared with white eyes ecstatically toward heaven.

But from the organ choir hundreds of boys leaned down to him, applauded him and laughed so that their laughter echoing drowned out the mad stammering, calling and whimpering below. Gigantic fiery roses fluttered down and spread a stupefying fragrance. The crowd gasped for breath.

Then from the altar sounded a gentle shepherd's melody. On the steps sat a naked youth playing the flute. A wondering hesitation. And already the boys sprang from the organ choir in great leaps as shaggy dogs among the crowd and drove them barking through the church.

The frightened ones wanted to flee. But the doors were firmly locked. More and more dogs pressed in on them, baring their teeth. People rolled on the floor, tried to save themselves onto the pews, to climb up to the windows, stumbled over each other, fell, picked themselves up, wrung their hands, gasped for air and wheezed.

Then the flute-playing fell silent. The youth, the dogs, the roses—all had vanished. The people looked at each other aghast. The church doors stood wide open.

No one dared say a word. Slowly they turned toward the exit and slunk crouched and without troubling about one another out onto the street.

### CHAPTER THIRTEEN: THE GREAT TRIAL

"Thank God," thought Ahlbeck when he was on the street again, "thank God that I have lost sight of Johannsen and Flamm. In what unworthy situations they beheld me! I jumped around like one possessed, knelt, whimpered, beat my forehead on the floor, ran from dogs, embraced market women. And all the others who saw me! There will certainly have been many more acquaintances in the church. If they spread that around in the city! I am lost. It can cost me my position. I'll end up in the madhouse. How will Johannsen take it? And Flamm? I can no longer look them in the eye... That music... What sort of music was that?... O God!... Oh well, the others carried on just as I did. They probably won't say anything about it. They'll be ashamed... But how did we come to do it at all?... In the church?!... Only now does it occur to me... The old Consistorial Councillor as a goat... the naked boy at the altar... the dogs... I think I'm going crazy... But that's nonsense. I must have fallen asleep in the church and dreamed... Of course... Any other explanation is impossible. That's what comes of listening to such stupid stories as my wife and Flamm told me!... But... but... it remains worrisome after all that I could imagine for a few minutes that all that really was true... Yes, yes... one grows old... Good that I only dreamed... But why did Flamm sneak away when he saw me fall asleep? That wasn't nice of him... God, there the clock is already striking ten from the tower. It's high time I betake myself to the Palace of Justice."

In the corridor of the courthouse he met Johannsen, who was very pale and had an unusual gleam in his watery eyes.

"Just imagine," said Johannsen a bit uncertainly, "I dreamed quite vividly of you this morning. It was in the cathedral. I dreamed that both of us were hopping about there with a crowd of fantastic figures. Most of it I've forgotten. But it is strange after all that one dreams oneself in such—hm—mildly put, compromising situations."

"Remarkable," replied the district judge, looking aside. "I too dreamed of you this morning. Something quite similar, incidentally."

The public prosecutor became embarrassed. After a while he asked hesitatingly:

"Yes, how do you actually explain that?"

"Very simply," answered Ahlbeck, whom the similarity of the dreams and the embarrassed manner of the prosecutor made somewhat suspicious, although he was even more firmly convinced than before that it had all been only a dream. "You know, the affair with the Subterraneans, the rumors from Rattenhusen... that eventually excites one and gradually ruins the nerves. We're not the youngest anymore, my dear Johannsen..."

And he sighed and shook his head thoughtfully. Johannsen was about to reply when a court attendant interrupted the conversation and reported that a strikingly dressed, beautiful lady with large, staring eyes urgently wished to speak to the public prosecutor before the session; she had an important communication for him.

The public prosecutor hesitated. Then he said quickly:

"Very well. I'll come at once!"

He took hasty leave of Ahlbeck and went. The latter watched him, lost in thought, and felt a light shudder run down his back.

"What is this only?" he murmured. "What is this only?"

The taking of evidence was closed. The defendant, a lust-murderer of about fifty, had confessed. Public Prosecutor Johannsen rose to give his speech.

He looked even paler than before and wiped the sweat from his forehead. Before he began, he looked around the hall once more. Everyone noticed how he started when his gaze fixed on a white-clad lady who sat among the spectators and did not take her eyes off him.

At last he began his speech. That is to say, one saw how he opened and closed his mouth incessantly, but no sound came from his throat. He himself seemed to notice nothing of it, for the gestures with which he accompanied his speech became ever more sweeping and passionate, the movements of his mouth ever faster.

Each of those present glanced furtively at his neighbor, but no one let on. They stared attentively at the silent speaker, who after a full hour closed with a grand gesture and sank exhausted into his chair.

The white lady from the audience called out a loud "Bravo" to him and clapped her hands. The rigidity fell from the listeners. They breathed with relief, likewise cried "Bravo," and applauded noisily.

District Judge Ahlbeck, who had sat there trembling the whole time and had not dared to interrupt the prosecutor, pulled himself together and rang his bell.

"Silence!" he called. "Or I'll have the spectators removed!... Please, Herr Councillor!"

The defense attorney, Councillor of Justice Musäus, rose.

"High Court! Gentlemen of the Jury," he began, but immediately fell silent with a horrified look. For a man who looked exactly like him, had the same bristling hair, the same flashing brown eyes, the same low, furrowed brow, indeed, who even wore the same clothing as he, had entered and hurried toward him with tripping steps.

"Fraud!" he cried in a somewhat hoarse voice that likewise resembled the Councillor's. "Fraud! A swindler whose features approximately match mine has put on similar clothing to what I wear and presumes to want to play my role here! What purpose he pursues with this is not yet clear to me. Gentlemen, you know me after all, convince yourselves! I thought so right away, that the Rattenhusen troublemakers would start their mischief here too. And indeed! Hardly do I enter the court building intending to go to the lawyers' room when the attendants hold me back and claim that Councillor Musäus is already in the courtroom and I am an impudent swindler whom they would lock up until the session was over. They have in fact done so. Only now have I succeeded in escaping, to expose here the impostor who usurps my place!"

Everyone looked in astonishment from one Councillor to the other. The two resembled each other so much that no difference was perceptible between them, except that the Councillor reviled as "impostor" sat there deathly pale and stammered unintelligible words, while his accuser continued with raised voice:

"You see, ladies and gentlemen, he is unable to bring forward anything in his defense!"

The audience made noise. Only after much ringing and threatening did the district judge succeed in restoring order and having the accused Councillor arrested, who dared no word of rejoinder and let himself be led away without resistance.

But the victorious Councillor mounted the speaker's platform and said:

"I have not attended the proceedings until now, but am so well acquainted with the case that I shall nevertheless undertake the defense of the accused."

The court was still so dazed by what had occurred that it permitted this unheard-of breach of custom without any objection. And the Councillor began:

"High Court! Gentlemen of the Jury! The accused has confessed. We are to pronounce judgment upon him. If we ask ourselves what drove him to his deed, there is only one answer: he wanted to procure himself a pleasure. He has done what we do daily."

The defendant sprang up. Among the rows of spectators arose an unwilling murmur. Ahlbeck rang and sharply interjected:

"Herr Councillor, I really must object..."

"He has done what we do daily," repeated Musäus unperturbed. "Some take pleasure in morality, others—in lust; some—in war, others in plain murder; some—in obeying the laws, others—in circumventing them; some—in the consciousness of their strength, others—in the enjoyment of their impotence; some—in doing business, others—in more harmless theft. Some take pleasure in everything in the world, some again in not being able to take pleasure in anything. But all seek their pleasure. What else has the defendant done?"

Here Ahlbeck could restrain himself no longer. Trembling with anger, he again interrupted the Councillor:

"Herr Councillor, this is no place for bad jokes!"

"Jokes?" asked the Councillor. "Never have I meant it more seriously than now."

"I ask you to stick to the matter, Herr Councillor," Ahlbeck flared up. "Otherwise I shall see myself compelled to withdraw your right to speak."

"As you wish, Herr District Judge," replied the Councillor calmly. "You have not thereby refuted what I have asserted. Your agitation proves how much I am in the right. You do not want to be robbed of the pleasure of sitting enthroned as judge. None of those present here wants to renounce it. It gives them all pleasure to decide over the life and death of a fellow man whose act of murder was at least honest and did not pass itself off as an act of supreme justice. Who has given you the power for this? Society. And of what does society consist? Of individual human beings, like him who sits there before you on the defendant's bench. Why has society made you judges? For the sake of justice? No, so as not to be unpleasantly disturbed in its pleasure. You have made yourselves a system of permitted pleasures, so that nothing frightening may startle you out of your calm and security. You who are society cower before this society. Your highest pleasure is to watch that no one raise his head and pursue a pleasure that you do not approve. Right? What do you call right? That individuals among you have the power to live their permitted pleasure, while the others, who work for your pleasure, cannot buy it and are excluded from it. The way you live, there must be Subterraneans. You yourselves have bred them. Those whom you have excluded now strive for the same power that you possess. They squat in dark vaults and ponder revenge. While you sit here in judgment, they sit in judgment upon you down there. And when they are strong enough, they will burst forth, flood your world, and sweep you from the face of the earth. By what right? By the same right by which you rule today. There is no difference between you and those down below. Your culture? You did not create it. It is the work of the elect, the yearning and the free, who have nothing to do with you. You have no part in it. Whether it is destroyed by the Subterraneans or desecrated by you is all the same: it exists neither for them nor for you. The Subterraneans will take revenge upon you and you upon them, until you have mutually torn each other to pieces and the earth again belongs to the pure!"

In the audience, which until now had listened grimly, a tumult broke out. Ahlbeck called shrilly:

"I withdraw your right to speak!"

But in place of the Councillor stood on the platform the same naked youth whom Ahlbeck had seen playing the flute in the cathedral.

"Do you recognize me?" called the youth. "An hour ago you danced in the church to my pipe. Shall I play for you again?"

Speechless, Ahlbeck fell back in his chair. The spectators had their mouths gaping open and did not stir. Only a few crossed themselves shyly. The public prosecutor was the first to recover his composure. He raised his arm and commanded:

"This is too much! Attendants, arrest this person!"

Then the white-clad lady in the spectators' gallery called loudly to him:

"Don't go too far, Johannsen! Think of the half hour you spent with me before the session in your office!"

All heads turned toward her. Johannsen turned pale and red and stammered:

"I... don't... know... what... that... is supposed... to mean..."

"I was irresistible, my darling," called the lady, "and you too were irresistible!"

And as by a magic stroke all clothing fell from Johannsen and the lady. The lady smiled and said:

"Thus we stood facing each other... And did not stand thus long..."

And while Johannsen stared at her wordlessly and Ahlbeck still grasped at the air with his hands as if pleading for help, old market women appeared from all corners of the hall, pointed fingers at the two and called:

"There they are! There they are! They danced with us in the church! Yoo-hoo!"

"Why are you so frightened, darling?" the lady began again. "The dancing in the church means nothing. More serious is that you thrust your paper knife into my breast when in your arms I transformed first into a boy and then into a sow. Don't you remember? Look here!"

From her breast protruded the ivory handle of a knife. Johannsen clutched at his throat. But the boy took him by the hand and said:

"Look at your hand. It is full of blood."

Twitching, Johannsen jerked his hand back, from which a large drop of blood ran to the floor. Then the defendant approached him and put his arm around his neck.

"Well then, Herr Public Prosecutor knows the situation," he said good-naturedly. "Give me a kiss, brother prosecutor!"

He embraced and kissed him. Johannsen had no other thought than: "A human being! A human being of flesh and blood! Thank God, a human being!" And he pressed the old lust-murderer passionately to his breast and kissed him with tears.

Then the boy turned to the jury:

"Well, gentlemen of the jury? Your verdict! Quick, quick, before your past too comes alive!"

"We are agreed, aren't we?" the foreman asked his companions with white lips, who nodded eagerly. "The defendant is free of guilt!" he proclaimed solemnly.

The boy and the lady clapped their hands.

"And now kiss each other! Kiss each other!" they cried. "Kiss each other all around! You are worthy of each other! You are brothers! You are one like the other! Kiss each other! Kiss each other!"

A surging, pressing and pushing ensued. Ahlbeck kissed the court attendant, the defendant, the jurors, the prosecutor, the spectators. And when he was done with all of them, he began again in breathless haste, as if he feared the horror from before might seize him again. The others followed his example. And in the wriggling confusion no one knew anymore whom he embraced and where he kissed. Like shrill inciting cries rang the "Kiss each other! Kiss each other!" of the lady and the youth between.

While in the hall everyone lay in each other's arms, all the bells of the city began to ring by themselves. In the streets people ran together and were at a loss. They besieged each other with questions, made assertions, contradicted, quarreled, screamed, raged—until a general brawl arose, to which the police with drawn swords had to put an end.

### CHAPTER FOURTEEN: THE UPRISING

In a short time the whole city knew of the incidents at the Palace of Justice. The rumors about the morning tumult in the cathedral were also confirmed more and more credibly. The unrest of the citizens grew from hour to hour and increased even more when news spread that the Subterraneans had made a breakout attempt at two places: it had been thwarted just in time, but that had cost several casualties, for the criminals had been armed with revolvers and rifles and had been openly supported by the other rebellious elements of the city.

It was told that the bodyguard regiment of Johann the Fourth, grandson of Walter the Second under whom Stuhlbrestenburg had been rebuilt, had been supplied with live ammunition and was standing ready in the barracks to march out at once. In the afternoon hours martial law was declared over the city.

As a consequence, the pleasure establishments were overfilled in the evening. The citizens, thanks to these precautionary measures of the government, began to feel secure again and seemed to want to compensate themselves for the fear they had endured. The excitement they had been in demanded relaxation.

The well-known "Rose Garden," which was located in one of the side streets leading to the great boulevard, was a castle-like building and bore its name not without reason. For its halls were not only decorated with fresh roses at all seasons, but also the red lamps on the ceilings had the shape of rosebuds and the girls dressed, according to an old tradition, only in rose-colored veils. The portal was adorned by two large gilded lions on which sat naked marble women astride.

This house, which made all citizen hearts of the land beat higher, was the secret pride of Stuhlbrestenburg: it was seen as the epitome of everything metropolitan, worldly, adventurous, and sinful.

There was above all the "great hall." With this hall there was a peculiar circumstance. No one was permitted to enter it in his ordinary clothing. In the reception hall there were small cloakrooms in which the guests completely undressed and threw over themselves colorful silk kimonos, which they could acquire, for purchase or loan, at the entrance. In the hall there were neither tables nor chairs. Everywhere, however, cushions and bolsters were irregularly scattered.

For it was established here that no one hid anything from the others. While some danced, the others rolled about in the cushions and gave themselves up before everyone's eyes to unreserved pleasures of love. Two large orchestras produced alternately an incessant, deafening din.

On this evening the rooms filled soon after eight o'clock. Earlier than usual dancing began. The guests of the great hall seemed seized by a frenzy. For when Ahlbeck, who after long, fearful wandering in the streets had resolved to save himself into pleasure, entered the hall in a yellow kimono, no one was dancing anymore. All lay panting in the cushions. The orchestra played a popular military march.

Ahlbeck was for a moment as if stunned by this sight. At least ten years had passed since he had last been in the "Rose Garden." What would he have had to seek there anymore? Inactive watching gave him no pleasure.

What had driven him here today was not quite clear to himself. Presumably he had so lost the feeling of self-assurance that he instinctively sought out the place where he had felt most at ease all his life. He probably wanted to convince himself that he himself was the one who had so often felt here, to use his favorite expression, "fully and completely as a human being."

"How long ago that already is..." he thought wearily, and his almost envious gaze fell upon the sixteen-year-old mayor's son, who was evidently here for the first time, for his gestures were still so hasty and clumsy, his behavior toward the girl who lay at his side so shy and respectful, as was the case with the Stuhlbrestenburg citizens only as long as they had not yet accustomed themselves to the "operation," as they called it.

"How long ago that already is," Ahlbeck said to himself wistfully, "that I myself was here for the first time. The boy there still has illusions. I too had illusions then. *Here* I believed them fulfilled, here was my paradise. The rest of life was not suited to fulfill these illusions. Even the 'Rose Garden' had not been able to do it... And that is also quite right. For it would make no reasonable sense... We are liberal people. One is proud here of the Rose Garden. Rightly so. In this way our life can run calmly, orderly, decently, and soberly. What threatens to burst this frame does not transform itself with us into foolish fantasies, does not become a complicated novel of the soul, does not degenerate into extravagant catastrophes. Here in the 'Rose Garden' is the place where we can discharge ourselves. Here we can live out everything that is lawless, raw, arbitrary, and rebellious in us in legally permitted form. How wise and far-sighted were our ancestors that they created these liberal institutions. From whom does the rebellious spirit in our community come? Only from those who, for so-called idealistic reasons, do not want to make use of this opportunity to live themselves out, or, for lack of money, cannot make use of it. That must change. The new age demands great things of us. At the next city council meeting I shall bring the motion that the city must call into life from its own means comfortable and hygienically impeccable institutions for free use by the poorer population. That will be expensive. But we shall have our peace. The social question is solved. And we shall manage the idealists—there are, thank God, only so few of them. They cannot be helped anyway..."

While Ahlbeck lost himself in these dreamy reflections, the music made a short pause. The guests started as from a dream.

"Hey, Halloo!" shouted a fat citizen in a red kimono who was hairy all over his body like an ape. "What's this? Music! Music!... Ah, good day, Herr District Judge, here again for once? How goes it after today's adventure? Dreadful, eh? But the police will restore order. And our splendid soldiers, no?... Want to recover a bit, Herr District Judge? Right into the pleasure! May I introduce you to my lady?... Miss Arabella—District Judge Ahlbeck... Nice breasts, eh? Regular sofa cushions!"

A sudden idea struck him. He raised himself up and called loudly into the hall:

"Most honored, most worthy! The music is pausing. And you too, it seems to me, have let a pause occur in your praiseworthy activity. I make a proposal!"

Everyone laughed.

"We sit here so merrily together and love each other so much. But let us not forget that not everyone can have it so good. There is a guest among us whom we have not met at this place for many years now, for age and gray hair... well, you understand me when you look at him, our honored District Judge Ahlbeck. But he too shall not come away empty. He too shall have his pleasure after today's strenuous day. Shall we, for his amusement, for our recreation, play a game in which he can participate? An innocent children's game! I shall count 'one—two—three.' And on 'three' the music strikes up and we throw ourselves upon our ladies and tickle them as long as they can stand it. The one among them who holds out longest will receive from all of us together a gift of a hundred thalers. Agreed?"

And he commanded:

"One—two—three—go!"

Thunderously the music began. At this moment the doors were flung open and a crowd of new arrivals pressed noisily in: boys in colorful garments, twenty, thirty, fifty, more and more. The guests looked up in astonishment. Already the whole hall was filled with the band. One of the boys pushed aside the helplessly resisting conductor and took his place. The music broke off abruptly.

"You girls!" called the boy into the hall, "what do you want from the beasts there who torment you? You are not in the world for them. For us! For us! You have dreamed of colorful wonders. We bring them to you. Whoever wants to come with us, cry: 'I want to leave!' And we shall carry you off into our kingdom!"

A short silence followed. And then arose from all sides a hasty, precipitate, longing cry:

"I want to leave! I want to leave! I want to leave!"

Then, as if opened by an invisible hand, the windows of the hall sprang wide open. Outside was dark night. The boys snatched at the crying girls, who threw themselves jubilantly at their breasts. Some were forcibly held back by the surprised and embittered guests. But hardly did the boys touch the resisting men with their fingertips when they were transformed into fat rats that ran squeaking back and forth on the floor.

The boy at the conductor's stand raised his arm and glared at the orchestra with wide-open eyes threateningly. A twitching went through the musicians. Whether they wanted to or not, they had to seize their instruments. They did not know what they were playing. Soft music sounded through the hall, became more silvery and faster and dissolved into bright racing, as if all the lights of heaven were circling through one another.

The boys and girls danced whirling. Already their feet no longer touched the floor. Already they floated without weight through the hall. A sweet outcry of the instruments. And to the dancers grew shimmering wings. As colorful butterflies they floated through the open windows far out into the star-clear night.

The boy who had conducted the music had vanished. The windows closed. All the guests had again their accustomed form and slept loudly snoring on the cushions and pillows. The orchestra played on willlessly until one musician after another dropped his instrument and they too fell asleep in their seats.

Meanwhile the performance in the Stuhlbrestenburg Court Theater had long since begun. The King was attending it in person in his box, to show the public, as was generally assumed, that there was no cause for any concern.

A new play was being performed that had as its author Clemens Rotbuch, the bank director of Rattenhusen who had meanwhile gone mad—a society drama, in a précieux-naturalistic style, which, as could be read in the *Stuhlbrestenburg Advertiser*, "brought to expression a sublimation and spiritualization of idealistic humanity, from the highest vantage point of the soul castigated all aberrations of an overwrought over-culture detached from the ground of state community, and opposed to the megalomaniacal freedom-intoxication of anarchist spirits the ethical depth and responsible commitment of socially minded citizenry."

The first two acts had been received with resounding applause. The audience was enthusiastic. On the stage was enacted what they had always been accustomed to picture to themselves in dreamy hours as an unattainable goal of life, so that they did not need to come to the thought of ever putting it into reality.

Quite secretly, however, they were somewhat bored. Some who sat at the side of their dolled-up, faded wives thought wistfully of past beautiful times in the quite differently constituted world of the "Rose Garden," or deliberated a good business deal to be concluded tomorrow. But they were ashamed of themselves for it and applauded these half-unconscious stirrings with not quite clear conscience all the more eagerly away, as the events on stage came to a head in a triumphant outbreak of bourgeois public spirit.

It was generally noticed that the young King sat quite bored in his box and only with difficulty suppressed his yawning. It was known that when he was still heir to the throne he had associated only with artists, adventurers, and degenerate representatives of the high nobility, yet he was in general much too tactful to give any emphasized expression to this taste of his after he had become King. He preferred to hold back altogether and limited himself to the necessary signing of documents. Affairs of state were repugnant to him—all the more repugnant the more he had to do with bourgeois society. Among the nobility and the simple folk he enjoyed certain sympathies, but to them too he was strange and a bit uncanny; no one knew what to make of him.

Just today, now, a King's word had been spread that had aroused much indignation among the citizens: when the first disquieting rumors about the incidents in the capital had come to his ears, he had, as was told, said with a pleased expression:

"Something different for once! Now they're getting at the fat ones. And the others will have their turn too."

It was also said that it had cost his ministers all the arts of their powers of persuasion to obtain from him the order for the troops to march out. Even today's theater visit he had undertaken only at the urgent wish of his chancellor.

The Stuhlbrestenburg citizens were consequently, notwithstanding their loyally submissive disposition, strongly put out and gave vent to their displeasure through intensified enthusiasm for the stage work that seemed to please their King so little.

In the intermissions an unusual excitement prevailed. There was eager dispute both about the strangely topical play and the fate of the unhappy author, as well as about the new events and the offensive position of the King.

When the curtain went up for the third time, tempers were heated to the utmost. The main scene of the play began: an argument between the hero of the drama, the director of the electric works, and his stepbrother, who had knocked about abroad for years and now as a member of a literary-anarchistic society was making unpleasant talk of himself—just at the moment when the director was to be appointed prime minister. This argument became ever more heated.

"But I say to you," preached the director mildly and eloquently, "you and your kind, you are the cancer of the land. You destroy what we have built, trample down what we have sown. I do not deny that with us not everything is as it should be. Modern economic life has unfortunately mechanized spiritual life, has made it soulless. But do not believe that by turning everything on its head you will awaken the dead soul to new life. If today's life is materialistic, it at least represents an ordered matter. What *you* want—is the materialistic chaos. But I say to you"—here he struggled with rising emotion in his voice—"that the soul is only seemingly dead. And if you ask me who shall awaken it to new life, then look!"—and he pointed with a grand gesture to his open safe. "Here is the awakener! The despised money, which, as you claim, is supposed to be to blame for all misery—gold will redeem the world. It has made all progress on earth possible in the first place. And we who unite it in our hands are the guardians of progress. If it were in the hands of all, it would be used up for the small facilitations and conveniences of life. In *our* hands, however, it means the creative power of the future. And when once in many hundreds of years the world will have progressed as far as we idealists, we practical idealists dream, when everything will have been achieved that we can and want to achieve with the help of this gold—then we shall no longer need to guard it, then may it belong to all. But history will thank us for our perseverance. Until then you will be able to wrest nothing from us with your sentimental fantasies; until then our tasks are more important than the small happiness of the many, to whom, incidentally, we are prepared to make all possible concessions according to reason and as far as is feasible... and now, brother, to whom I hereby offer a position on the board of directors of the electrical company—I ask you for the last time: do you still want to stand in my way?"

The audience listened with interest, approval, and fascination. A breathless silence ensued. One could distinctly hear the King clearing his throat in his box. Some turned around indignantly and even hissed.

Then on the stage the door to the director's office opened and two white-clad boys entered. A rustling went through the rows of spectators. Many had already read the play beforehand; the boys did not appear in it. What did their appearance mean?

While they stood there motionless, the stage transformed into a luxuriant garden. The director of the electric works, who was just about to begin a new speech, opened his mouth, gesticulated with his arms, and did not know what to do. The boys looked at him sharply and piercingly. Then his legs began to shrink, became thinner and shorter and finally disappeared entirely into his belly, which swelled into a yellow ball on which the round head sat tiny and helpless. The ball rose from the ground and floated low over the stage. One boy gave it a push and hurled it laughing to the other, who threw it back, and back and forth flew the luminous ball, in which now even the ever-smaller head of the electricity director had vanished.

From the audience rose indignant protests and catcalls. People stamped, hissed, and laughed. A high voice cried:

"This goes too far! We won't put up with this anymore! One isn't safe anywhere anymore!"

Then one boy gave the ball such a powerful push that it flew into the King's box. The King laughed aloud and threw it back to the boys on the stage. But in flying the ball burst with a loud bang. At the same time one saw distinctly how it took on again in the air the form of the director, from whose torn belly a rain of gold pieces, mixed with blood, poured into the audience. A second later an empty skin fell slapping to the floor.

The King leaned over the railing of his box and applauded enthusiastically.

Then behind him from the darkness of the box two other boys appeared, set a crown on his head, put scepter and orb into his hand, and laid an ermine cloak around his shoulders.

Over the heads of the spectators a flower bridge shot from the stage across to the royal box. And the boys took the King by the hands and stepped with him onto the swaying bridge. He let himself be led by them willlessly.

The agitated theatergoers looked up at him with dull horror. They saw that his eyes were wide and radiantly open. His slender figure bent a little forward as under a burden. Around his lips played a happy smile. Slowly and gropingly he took step after step.

No one stirred in the hall. The court marshal, who had been near to fainting, used this silence and tried to save the situation. He stepped forward and called ringingly into the audience:

"His Majesty, our most gracious King! Hurrah! Hurrah! Hurrah!"

The orchestra blew a fanfare. The audience felt relieved. It understood nothing, but could let off steam by shouting. Some already began to sing the national anthem. Others joined in. Then the King stumbled. The crown fell from his head, right into the gaping crowd. They pressed forward to pick it up. It was made of cardboard.

A tumult arose. Some laughed and shouted, others whistled and stamped. Only a few endeavored undiscouraged to sing the national anthem to the end.

Then smoke clouds rose from all corners. Outside dull noises were heard. Flames shot up. The theater was burning.

The boys and the King had vanished in an instant. The doors were flung open from outside. Dark figures armed with hatchets, pistols, and sabers pressed in.

For half a second everything stopped. Then one screamed out in animal fear:

"The Subterraneans! The Subterraneans! They have broken out! The Subterr—" but he got no further: a hatchet blow split his skull.

The slaughter began. The audience was encircled and pressed together so that they could not move. From all sides fell saber and hatchet blows. Between them bright shots crackled. One after another sank hoarsely bellowing to the floor.

The smoke grew thicker and thicker. But the Subterraneans paid no heed to it. They could no longer retreat either. From outside new masses pressed in. No one heard the other. Many had seized each other by the throats and strangled each other to death.

Already flaming beams came loose and fell crackling onto the twitching bloody heap. The roaring increased. The masses pressed apart. A frightful crash. And the whole building burst, collapsed, and buried all present under its smoking ruins.

Throughout the whole city a bitter struggle was going on. No one knew anymore against whom he was fighting. In senseless fear people fell upon one another.

In the marketplace the battle raged most fiercely. Thousandfold cries filled the air. Then a bright voice sounded out of the tumult. A dark figure had swung itself onto the roof of a stationary streetcar and called:

"Friends, hear me, hold back!"

Those standing nearest paused and listened.

"Hold back!" called the figure. "Reflect!"

More and more people paused and listened. It was as if the force of these calls gradually brought the raging ones back to themselves. One called it to another. Slowly a fearful silence spread.

"Friends," the figure continued, "reflect! You are only afraid of each other, that is why you murder each other. The old order makes you enemies, so create a new one! Do not forget who your true enemies are. It is the strange boys. They hide themselves in all forms. They take on your own form. Citizens, Subterraneans! You hate each other, but you know each other. But who are the boys? Who knows them? Where do they come from? What do they want? Whatever goals you may achieve, they dissolve what has been achieved and conjure up new things before you. Follow them—and you will know no peace anymore. The ground will rock beneath your feet. All life will vanish from you, all order, all will. A whirlwind will carry you off, from which you can never free yourselves again. None of you will know the other anymore. None will know himself anymore. All will merge into all forms. No form will remain. Madness will tear you apart in horror, lust, and dread!"

The crowd had at first followed the speaker as if frozen, but from minute to minute grew more restless again. Dull shouts, wild exclamations, curses, imprecations, questions interrupted him:

"The boys! The boys! Who are the boys? Who are you? Seek the boys! Kill them! No, kill that dog up there! He is a traitor! The boys! Where? Where?"

The figure on the wagon roof stretched out its hands again.

"Friends," it began anew, "you want to reach God, the new God, so that from you, from your will, your longing, your work he may radiate into the world. You want the new form of your life. You want a holy order, the holy order of your work. It lies in you, in your longing, your work itself. I only show it to you. I tell you what you yourselves feel. I teach you what you yourselves want. I give you the laws that you impose upon yourselves. I and my kind, we want to be brothers, helpers, healers to you. We have thought for you, suffered for you, overcome ourselves for you. We were the secret masters and now want to be your servants!"

A moonbeam fell on the figure. The crowd saw in the white icy light a beardless, sharp-cut, withered face that ended in a pointed, energetic chin. The mouth was thin and broad. The nose narrow and curved. The eyes as of bright, transparent stones. Over a mighty forehead of radiant whiteness fell blond, wavy hair.

A band of companions hovered around the speaker and shone in unearthly light. Their forms were transparent and flashed like shimmering glass. The light that they radiated ran over the heads of the crowd and dipped the whole square in milky gleam. The crowd shuddered and swayed, laughed and wept. Sinking to their knees, they threw their hands up to the radiant ones and called:

"Teach us! Stay with us! Do not leave us! Help us!"

"We want to help you!" answered the figure, and its voice sounded like a bell. "We want to help you if you help yourselves. Forget that longing that tears you from one another, that lures you into the unlimited. Forget your dreams! Live with one another, for one another, in one another—and your wild evil dreams will dissolve into nothing. Be knowing ones! Tolerate no darkness, no confused sleep, no self-escape! Never again dive into the old dark wells! Then your sleep will be refreshment to you and no longer a flight into another kingdom into which your unknown wishes and cravings carry you off! Do not hunger for an infinity that does not exist! By that you lure the boys here who destroy and annihilate you. Forget them—and they lose their power, they become serviceable to you, they become like you, their kingdom is over!"

"The boys! Where are the boys?" it screamed in the crowd. "Kill them! Kill the dogs! We want to be brothers! We want to live! We tolerate no madness among us! Kill them!"

"Do not touch them!" called the glass figure. "You can do nothing to them as long as you have not recognized them!"

But no one listened anymore. For in the middle of the square a high flame blazed up and in its red light a band of naked boys pressed together. In an instant it became deathly still.

Then a boy stepped forward and spoke:

"To us, whoever wants to be free! Let the others build their towers into heaven! Let them force heaven down upon the earth! Let them freeze in order, work, and happiness! To us, whoever loves the flame and eternal transformation! Into *our* night when *your* day suffocates! Into *our* kingdom when *your kingdom* is destroyed!"

The naked band struck up a wild, calling song. A trembling went through the crowd. Then everyone heard from the air a new song mingling with the wild song. The glass lords sang a strict, solemn tune that tried to force the song of the naked ones into *their* rhythm. The crowd fell into roaring motion:

"To the lords! To the lords! They help us! The new God! Worship! Worship! Kill the boys! Sacrifice! Sacrifice!"

Senseless, they seized their weapons and threw themselves upon the boys. A gust of wind drove into the flame. It swelled up, a blazing giant sail on a fire-boat that lifted itself with the singing band into the air and slowly floated above the heads of the raging ones.

"Shoot them down! Shoot them down! Don't let them escape!" it shrieked below. Commands sounded. The cannons in the marketplace were aimed. More commands. The shots thundered off. And the fire-boat sprayed apart into sparks. Millions of roses sank fluttering down onto the square and filled the air with dark, breath-taking fragrance.

## THE KINGDOM

### CHAPTER FIFTEEN: THE TRANSFORMATIONS OF LOVE

The evening earth burned hot beneath his feet. Rocks surged wavering upward. Blooming umbels hung from the shrubs and brushed his neck. The river flowed in the light rhythm of his blood. In the crowns of the trees he heard his name rustling.

Li felt how the path over which he walked was a living body. Eyes gazed up at him from the ground. He shuddered and took a step sideways. But again his foot struck breathing flesh.

He turned around: the trees behind him had become naked bodies. He looked upward: from the sky a radiant face inclined toward him.

The underbrush reached for him with waving arms. From all sides warm wind touched him. From the river, from the trees, from the earth, from the air, fading voices called to him.

Still he hesitated. Still he held his breath in apprehension. Yet already his limbs were loosening. He raised his hands and let himself fall with outstretched arms.

Foaming, blossoms broke from the ground and grew rampant over him in long tendrils. The waves of the river stepped sluggishly from their bed, rose higher, and flooded, softly surging, ever closer to his resting place in pauses. He felt how the pauses grew shorter. Not long, and the waves touched his limbs.

In red radiance, last rays of sunlight broke through rustling branches and showered him with light. Slowly darkening, it poured over him, a cascade of fluttering hair. And always wide-open eyes stared at him, scorching him, burning only brighter the more the hair lost its gleam.

Larger and more rushing came the waves of the river. They embraced him and pressed softly against his feverish skin. They lifted him cool from the earth. They seized him and swept him away.

The sun had sunk. Stars scattered in fleeting images across the sky. Li's body stretched and grew into space. No longer did he float along in the river: all rivers roared through his heart. No longer did he feel the loving breath of the earth: the earth breathed from his breast. No longer did the trees reach fleshy branches toward him: his arms sprouted from all trees into the darkness.

He breathed deeply. And rivers, wind, trees, and earth tore themselves painfully from his body: he floated. Deep below he saw his shadow fall over lands and seas.

Then a cry rose from the earth to him. And he tumbled plunging down again into blooming ground. Lips pressed wildly to his mouth. Beneath hanging undergrowth he lay in entwining arms. A delicate form drew him gently to her breast. Their limbs intertwined trembling.

Li felt nothing but the shuddering of skin against his own, the trembling of lips on his mouth, the twitching of arms around his neck. He heard the beating of the other heart and could no longer distinguish it from his own. He saw the strange eyes widen ever more painfully and darkly and believed he was gazing into his own eyes.

"Who are you?" he whispered, "where do you come from?"

Then a wind carried them away in rocking rush. Blue darkness lightened. Mists rose and swelled. A hall grew around them with white columns. Garlands of flowers wreathed their bed. Outside moon-lit sea glittered. From afar singing echoed over to them. Through the endlessness of the forest of columns a crowd of dancers moved.

They embraced more violently. Ever more furious became the play of their unleashed bodies. Li felt how the form in his arms swelled and grew. A woman clasped him with mighty limbs. Fearful lust raced breathlessly through his blood.

The white hall reddened and filled with smoky light. Countless small flames crackled around the columns.

"Who are you?" Li whispered anew, "where do you come from?"

Then the columns dissolved in darkly blazing haze. Gloomy walls loomed, coursed by fleeting glitter. From the niches sounded, now nearer, now farther, the clashing of cymbals and soft drum rolls.

Li's body contorted and transformed. He too had become a woman and pressed more sluggishly and more ardently against his twitching bed-companion, who was already melting and dissolving into new form: a bronze-colored giant weighed upon Li with broad chest and strong-boned arms. His face was threateningly distorted, his mouth gaping wide, his gleaming teeth glimmered between black lips, in his slit eye-sockets burned unfathomable eyes.

Tall figures with bald skulls and whitish slit eyes stepped in golden mantles from the walls. They struck up a monotonous, goading song. At the highest tone, which they held endlessly long, they raised their swords. The face above Li looked even more threatening and darker.

"Who are you?" Li whispered without voice, "where do you come from?"

But this was no longer the yellow giant he was asking: a brown countenance with furrowed features and thin lips smiled at him. Delicate long-fingered hands caressed him, seeming strangely withered and cool.

One transformation tore another after it. Li pressed himself, dissolving, against a Negress with thick lips, against a gaily feathered Indian, against a dark girl painted with yellow suns.

At times it seemed they were shooting along in a boat on a river. Sudden sunlight alternated with darkness and shimmering twilights. Blazing, wave-foam sprayed around them.

In ever new embraces he knew himself, in ever new spaces and bodies. Now he was a slave, kissed by a glittering emperor in a hot tent, now a camp whore, spread upon a drum, gasping under the bodies of soldiers reeking of blood, now a priest in the fragrant bed of a fragile woman.

Whirlwinds drove him through billowing scents. It grew dark around him. He could no longer distinguish anything.

Muffled voices made him look up. And once more he saw himself amid uncertainly glittering temple walls. From the niches sounded again the clashing of cymbals and drum rolls. Motionless the slit-eyed priests stood at the walls. He lay, with the beloved form in his arms, bound by cords on the altar stone. They were a small, black-haired peasant couple, looking about with tormented animal eyes, bleeding from many wounds. Mighty domes vaulted above them. Behind the altar the yellow giant loomed as a bronze idol into the swaying space. The priests circled them with raised swords. Li screamed in nameless terror. Then the swords hissed down.

Li saw his blood spray up. Everything became red vapor. And from the vapor rose a thousand-limbed primeval forest with swaying giant trees, lush creeping vines, and man-high ferns. Animal roars pressed forth from the thickets.

A panther struck his claws into Li's flesh, who was himself a hissing wild cat. Millions of colorful birds screeched above them in the scorching wind. A last shriek. And Li scattered into the void. He knew nothing more of himself.

In raging descent he found himself again. In one second he sank through all the spaces through which his play had driven him. Festive songs echoed from afar. Blue darkness lightened. Mists rose and swelled. Garlands of flowers wreathed the bed. Through the endlessness of the forest of columns moved crowds of dancers.

Then an immense radiance broke in. Sunlight poured from circling blue. On cloudy cushions Li awoke. Fo slept at his side. His breath went calm and free. His hair tongued wildly around his bright forehead. From his face came a clear shining. His lips twitched now and then as in slight pain. His slender body lay in the icy morning light pure and white, bathed by blue trickling waves of fragrance, and was of such grace that Li, shaken, broke into tears.

Fo opened his eyes, beheld Li, took his face between his hands and kissed him on the forehead.

They looked about them. From all clouds awakened companions stretched into the young morning.

### CHAPTER SIXTEEN: THE DOWNFALL

They raised their hands in greeting to the light. Then a ringing storm of flute-cries and horn-calls rushed through space. When the boys heard it, a rustling went through their ranks.

"The storm!" cried Fo, "the storm from over there. We hear it again. We have arrived. The kingdom is near!"

He stood with his back to the sun. A gust of wind rushed into his hair. It spread like rays across the whole sky.

"The storm! The storm!" cried the boys, "the kingdom is near, we are home!"

"We are home!" Fo repeated, "we die into the kingdom. We plunge into its black springs to bloom anew into the world!"

And he opened his arms, looked at his companions ardently, and continued solemnly:

"Time sinks. Space scatters. Form expires."

The boys crowded around him and trembled. Fo slowly let his arms sink. His limbs began to twitch painfully. And already the whole band was shaken by convulsions. Their faces suddenly seemed ancient and withered, their hair white, their eyes blind, their skin slack, their hands thin and aged. All looked at Fo, who bent as under a heavy burden.

And like a mist a form dissolved out of him, a second, a third followed, more and more he released from himself. They floated fluttering and whispering back and forth and breathed away into emptiness.

From each of the boys, who writhed in pain, emerged in long rows airy forms: girls with rigid smiles, old men with mad gazes, armored ones in black plates, angels with heavy blood-rimmed wings, men in robes, black coats, colorful doublets and tailcoats, yellow, black, red warriors in wild adornment.

Li saw hundreds of faces floating away from him: he recognized priests, soldiers, poets, and heroes from all times and lands, he beheld all the forms into which he himself had transformed since he had dwelt among the boys: everything he had loved, everything he had feared, everything he had admired, everything he had been, streamed from him and departed. In the sky, in racing change, dimly clouded, immense landscapes dissolved, fiery cities full of swarming shadows, ancient dark villages, high pointed cathedrals, mysteriously glowing castles, crumbling fortresses; ever new streets and buildings glimmered palely and vanished.

Li was tortured by unbearable pain. He howled out and heard the others howl in agony too. Their groaning mingled with the whispering of the airy forms and shrilly drowned out the ringing storm.

With each shadow that departed from the boys, their bodies became more insubstantial, their movements weaker, their cries softer. They began to glow with a gentle inner light. But still the procession would not end, still new forms dissolved out of them. All power and all knowledge died in them in circling dissolution. Powerless and will-less they surrendered to the fleeing flood of forms. Their rigid eyes fixed on Fo, they did not see how the distant glow of home veiled itself with haze and shattered and vanished, did not feel how foreign air streamed hostile around them, dull heaviness paralyzed them. Who had bound them in a spell? Who had approached to fetter the defenseless blind?

A thunder from below made them start in fear. The clouds on which they floated had descended. Earth rose to meet them. They sank deeper and finally found themselves in a moldering grove. They wanted to scream and could not. Only Fo stammered tonelessly with bluish lips:

"This... is... not... the... kingdom... this... is... not... the... kingdom..."

With all their strength they pulled themselves together and froze before the last forms could stream out of them. Sluggishly swamp grew around them and swallowed the decaying trunks of the grove.

Fo's eyelids fell heavy over his eyes. Li saw how he collapsed to the ground but could not move a limb to hurry to his aid.

From steaming green, colorful birds whirred screeching into the low sky and fluttered madly back and forth.

Then strangers emerged amid the band. They had crept silently up to the frozen ones, laid their arms around their necks and inclined toward them to kiss them.

In the eyes of some boys a nameless horror kindled. Flying fear loosened their weakened limbs, they could move their arms again and thrust the strangers back.

But the others sank helplessly to their breasts and let themselves be kissed. One of the strangers also approached the sleeping Fo and bent down to him. Li screamed:

"Awake! Awake!"

But Fo did not hear him. The stranger inclined deeper over him. Yet scarcely had he touched him with his hands when Fo, with a twitching effort, tore his eyes open and cried with a terrible voice:

"Hold back! Hold back! Look about you! Save yourselves! Hold back!"

And he sprang up and braced his limbs. With flashing eyes he wrote signs in the air with both arms and uttered shrieking dark words.

Then a rushing arose above him. From drifting mists the forms that the band had released from themselves condensed again and filled the air with lamenting whimpering.

The few boys who had resisted the strangers shuddered back at the sight of the shadows. But Fo looked at them with an expression so full of grieving love that they obediently bowed their heads. A smile glided over their aged features. They gathered around him and breathed deeply. And while a convulsion tore their bodies even more dreadfully than before, they became one again with the forms.

They were young again as before.

Around those among them who had been kissed by the strangers, the lamenting shadows also drifted.

"To me! To me!" Fo's voice rang out.

But it was too late. The victims sunk in death-like sleep did not hear. And the strangers blew into the wandering shadows so that they scattered and dissolved into air. Without looking back, they strode away and carried their prisoners with them. As they strode away their bodies became transparent like glass.

---

One evening Li found himself on a vast ice-plain.

"What is happening to us?" he said to himself, "since the strangers blocked our way to the kingdom, something has come over us. We lose ourselves randomly in rampant entanglements. What rises in us from forgotten depths? We no longer recognize ourselves. Our band scatters. Only rarely now do we find each other again in the frenzy that has seized us..."

The sun shone red. A gust of wind came and swept all the snow away so that it flew up crackling. The ice was smooth as a mirror. He was freezing.

All at once he recognized nearby shores, church towers, houses, roofs, dams: he was on the frozen river of his native town Schimmelberg, near the old house on Löwengracht where he had grown up.

The ice was full of laughing and chattering people. Colorful push-sleds, yellow, green, red, blue, violet, raced past him.

A large red sled stopped right in front of him. It was shaped like an animal. At the front a dragon's head adorned it. It bore the features of the old watchmaker of Schimmelberg, Louis Lindenroth. He had sad eyes and said to Li in a weary voice:

"Melchior, don't you recognize me?"

As if struck by lightning, Li swayed when he heard himself called by his old name, which he had deeply forgotten.

But the watchmaker's head continued:

"Don't hang yourself, like I did, old boy. It's pointless anyway. Now I have to serve here on a stupid sled as a dragon's head, that's all that's left of me. Do better than I did. Here, I'll lend you five thalers. Drink yourself silly!"

He laughed briefly, wobbled back and forth a few more times and froze. Now it was no longer a dragon's head but the head of a tomcat with screwed-up eyes and long drawn-out whiskers.

Li knew that he himself was that Melchior whom the old watchmaker had addressed, that today his fifteenth birthday had been celebrated and he had been given a new coat with a black, long-haired fur collar.

Then the sled-driver, muffled in furs, called to him:

"Get in, Melchior," he said, "you're freezing to death!"

It was the voice of his school friend Heinrich Wunderlich. Li got in and sat down. Heinrich leaned from behind close over his face. Li saw his eyes close above him. They were frighteningly large and full of tears. Li too wept.

Then the ice burst beneath them with a dull crack. But already they were sitting in a small rowboat and it was hot summer. Heinrich rowed. His face was sun-burned; his chest beneath the open white shirt gleamed brown. They rowed toward Li's father's house, whose front faced the river.

Li clambered ashore. The boat with Heinrich had vanished. In the garden surrounding the house he saw his early-departed brothers merrily wrestling with one another. Henriette Karlsen sat on a bench and was sketching.

Scarcely had Li entered the garden when no one was to be seen anymore. Voices called him from the lilac bushes. He walked toward the shrubs and pressed into their darkness. The branches closed behind him. In the twilight sat Otto von Lobe, half-naked with crossed legs, playing sluggishly on a guitar. He had grown even more delicate and looked sick and careworn. His white lips trembled. His eyes were closed. When he heard Li coming, he opened them, beheld him and said sadly:

"The ship left long ago and I didn't come along. I missed it. Now I have to play the guitar here for the Old One!"

And he pointed with his long, thin index finger, on which sat a ring with a large blood-red stone, at a spot in the dusky branches.

Li saw an old man rising from the earth, buried up to his armpits. It was his father.

"Where am I?" he thought, "from where does all this emerge? What tangles together here?"

His father looked at him kindly, nodded to him and said:

"Now the time is almost up. Now my ship will come. Then we travel away, to the new land, to the star-land!"

While he spoke, Li noticed how the whole thicket was surrounded by tensely listening faces. He screamed fearfully:

"You must not go! You must not go!"

Then his father flew up as a black bird. In the heights he became more and more colorful. Many hundreds of birds joined him. They had human voices and screeched all at once:

"We have returned! We always return!"

But before Li could collect himself, another world already surrounded him. He stood at the quay of a great harbor. White gulls circled him with whistling wing-beats. A broad shadow fell on the shore. Li started. An enormous sailing ship had docked. Many travelers went ashore. But at the ship's prow stood Fo and waved to Li with a white handkerchief. And again the air was full of colorful birds. They screeched:

"We have returned! We always return!"

Li did not know how it had happened. But he was riding with Fo on two black horses over withered stubble fields. Night sank down. The cloudless sky was full of stars.

Fo called to Li:

"Ride faster! Ride faster! Otherwise we'll be too late!"

The ride became more breathless. The horses pressed together. Fo and Li touched, laid their arms around each other's shoulders and kissed.

For the first time at a touch from Fo, Li was seized by an inexplicable fear. He wanted to free himself from his embrace. But in the sky a small black cloud grew, folded together like a cloth and fell over them.

Fo cried:

"Breathe calmly!"

And the cloth rose again, fluttered up and dissolved. All around sandy seashore spread. Tall beach-grasses rustled around them. And Fo said:

"We are saved! We can travel! There lies our boat!"

They hurried to the shore and pushed a fishing boat into the sea.

At the same moment, quite suddenly, without dawn, the sun rose. And Li recognized that the ferryman who was rowing him away standing was not Fo, but the glassily shimmering form of Ulrich von Spät.

Li could only cry out. Then night closed around him.

### CHAPTER SEVENTEEN: THE RETURN

The garden was surrounded by a high wall. The gates were strictly guarded. The white buildings had a treacherously reassuring appearance.

On the green bench before the main building always sat four men in blue-striped linen smocks, smiling to themselves. Their eyes seemed to see nothing. From time to time they nodded their heads, grasped at the air as if trying to catch something, and bleated like goats.

Between two tall firs stood an old woman with a yellow, shriveled face and long hanging gray hair. She stepped from one foot to the other, so that she constantly swayed back and forth, and murmured incomprehensible words. Only when an attendant in a white apron passed by did she interrupt herself and look at him lurking. Sometimes she ran silently after him and seized him from behind with clawed fingers at the throat. Then other attendants immediately rushed over, tore her away, overpowered her, and dragged the loudly howling woman into one of the white buildings.

In the middle of the garden, on the large lawn surrounded by old trees, the most varied people in blue-striped smocks romped about.

One of them danced the whole day on the same spot and stamped the ground in furious pleasure while singing shrilly.

Some rolled restlessly back and forth in the grass or crouched there dull and apathetic. Others crept from group to group, threw themselves now upon this one, now upon that, tried to knock them down and beat them or to embrace and kiss them. When the attendants, who appeared everywhere unexpectedly among them, noticed anything of the sort, they separated them by force.

An old man with long hair and rigid eyes walked calmly back and forth. A still young, delicate and beautiful woman approached him. She carried a framed picture in her hands. At first she hesitated a while and looked at the old man mistrustfully. Then she smiled happily and sank to her knees before him.

"You are the great God!" she said pleadingly, "you walk among us poor ones to stand by us and do us good. Be merciful to me! Help me! You know everything. They killed my husband. Nothing is left to me of him but his beard. I cut it from his corpse and put it under glass and frame. Here it is. Here."

And she held up to him the picture she carried with her. The old man cast a fleeting glance at it. Under the glass he truly beheld a thick full beard of deep black color pasted on gold paper.

"Help me!" the woman continued weeping and clutched the old man's knees, "good God, help me! Make my husband alive again. I cannot live without him. I am always so afraid, I am so alone. Behind all the trees they lie in wait for me. Help me!"

The old man took the picture from her hand, contemplated it shaking his head, and gave it back to her in silence. She pressed it passionately to her lips and suddenly let out a wild shriek:

"It's him! It's him! You've made him alive again. My husband is back. He is back!" And she let the picture fall and reached out with both arms before her as if she were embracing someone, kissed into the empty air, broke into a neighing laughter, made some obscene gestures with her torso, and danced spinning away.

But the others had heard her shrieking and came running curiously. And already some were bending their knees before the old man, kissing the dust from his shoes and crying:

"God have mercy on us! Save us!" But they were drowned out by a tall young man who bellowed at them with burning anger:

"I am the Lord your God! You shall have no other gods beside me! Ask, and it shall be given unto you! Knock, and it shall be opened unto you!"

And the whole crowd began to crow, grunt, bark, and howl. They clasped hands and danced, swinging their legs, around the old man, but soon stopped in horror, for one of them had fallen to the ground and convulsively gasped:

"The sun is setting! It's growing dark! The eternal night! They're coming with tongs! Help! Mercy! Fire! Eternal fire!"

His screams dissolved into sobbing laughter.

"Dancing!" he whimpered, "we want to dance, dance on the fire! We are dancing bears! Yahoo!"

And he pulled himself up and stomped clumsily up and down, continuously weeping, babbling, and laughing.

Meanwhile a grim-looking bald man had taken the old man under the arm and said urgently:

"Let's leave the rabble! Come, there's a bench over there. I must speak with you!"

The old man followed him. They sat down in the shadow of the high garden wall.

"Listen to me calmly!" said the bald man, "why should we continue to misunderstand each other? Why do we lie in wait for each other? It's all senseless!"

"But I'm not lying in wait for you at all," replied the old man.

"Yes, you were, I know it, I felt it, from the very first day you came here. But let's leave that! I am the Emperor, as you know. But I do not wish to be recognized. I always live in different forms. I have a thousand faces. Nevertheless I was recognized by you. You knew at once who I am. But I also know who you are. You are a great lord, a very great lord. I will name no names. But I know you. Why should we live in enmity? We can come to terms. Let us divide! You take the southern hemisphere and I the northern. I am even ready to add part of the northern to your southern. For I admit the people there are less intelligent. But all the more pleasant to rule, you know, less rebellious. Let us unite. I will agree to any proposal you make. Would you perhaps like the north? Yes? Take it! The south is enough for me too. I don't care. But... don't persecute me anymore! Let us unite! It is high time! Otherwise everything will grow over our heads. We must exterminate mankind in time, before there are too many. But they mustn't notice anything beforehand, or they'll imprison us. We want to make the earth a paradise again, unfortunately everything has become so ugly. We will leave a few women alive and beget new people with them... But carefully, for God's sake, carefully! Don't blab anything! We must proceed quite in secret, quite in secret!... Will you?"

He stretched his hand toward him. But the old man answered:

"I don't understand you!"

"Oh, you don't want to?" hissed the bald one. "You want everything for yourself alone? You want to kill me?... Now I know you. But watch out, watch out! I am vigilant. I know, I know..."

Meanwhile he looked around fearfully on all sides. From afar a white figure approached. When he caught sight of it, he cried out sharply and ran away in long leaps, terrified.

The white one came nearer and stopped before the old man, who was looking sadly after the one who had run away.

"Well, how goes it?" he asked.

The old man cleared his throat and said:

"Thank you, Doctor. But I repeat my request. Discharge me."

"I came to you," said the doctor, "to speak with you about that."

"I can't endure it here any longer, Doctor," the old man continued, "everything is too true here. Out there it hides itself under a feigned deeper meaning. Here life plays itself out naked before us. I would like to go to my work. I believe I shall trouble no one."

The doctor thought for a moment, then said:

"Good, but let me speak openly with you first. I know you are actually completely cured. Your outstanding chemical investigations, which you have conducted here before our eyes, prove that incontrovertibly. I will not try to talk you out of your fixed idea that you are that Dr. Melchior van Lindenhuis from Schimmelberg who disappeared a hundred years ago. I consider it impossible to dissuade you from that. But since you are otherwise, as I said, completely healthy, nothing stands in the way of your discharge. The wild fantasies in which you lived when you were found a year ago in a fishing boat drifting on the open sea have vanished, nothing of them has remained in your memory. Unfortunately you also cannot recall your real name. To accommodate you, we will therefore petition the authorities that you be permitted to use the name Melchior van Lindenhuis. The state university, which is keenly interested in your work, will continue to look after you."

Three days later Melchior was discharged.

---

Years passed. Melchior had long been a professor at the university. His fame had spread quickly. Far beyond the borders of the land he was revered as the foremost authority in his field. His health seemed indestructible. He worked day and night without tiring. His scholarly activity was the only thing with which he occupied himself. To all else he had died.

His colleagues and students were far too tactful to remind him with even a word of the circumstances under which he had appeared in the city. They were precisely informed about which fixed idea he suffered from, and took care not to touch upon it. And Melchior himself never circled in his thoughts around these things, they were veiled for him as with a dense fog, into which he had no desire to penetrate.

When he walked through the streets, his eyes seemed to look only inward. He always took the same way from his dwelling to the university. He made no other walks.

Only sometimes he felt seized by a tormenting restlessness he could not explain to himself. But he always overcame it quickly and took up his work with redoubled zeal.

One afternoon, however, something strange happened to him. On the main street he saw a young person walking before him, whose supple form and springing gait captivated him. He quickened his steps to catch up with him and see his face. As he passed him, the young person glanced fleetingly at him. Melchior was shaken. He lifted his hat and said in a faltering voice:

"Allow me to introduce myself, my name is Melchior van Lindenhuis."

The young person looked astonished.

"Ah, Professor," he said, "it is an honor. I am Walter Mahr, studiosus juris. How may I serve you?"

Melchior became embarrassed.

"I don't quite know," he replied, "how to explain this to you. When I saw you walking before me, a dark memory rose in me. I therefore followed you. And now that I see your face, the first impression has intensified. It seems to me I must have seen you somewhere already, much earlier."

Mahr made a startled gesture, but immediately collected himself and said:

"I wouldn't know where that could have been. I was born here and grew up here. I have never left the city. And you have only been here for three years."

"And yet, and yet, dear Mahr," Melchior interrupted him, "one should never swear to anything. But we have arrived at my door. Come up to my rooms for a few minutes. I would like to chat with you a little."

After they had taken seats in Melchior's study, they were silent for a while. At last Mahr began somewhat hesitatingly:

"I must make a confession to you, Professor, but when you told me earlier that you must have seen me somewhere much earlier, I was startled, for as a boy I always dreamed of a face that was just like yours, only much younger."

"Yes, one dreams many things," Melchior interjected, "I too will have dreamed of you."

"I dreamed," Mahr continued, "that this face looked in at my window and called me loudly. Strangely—the voice too was just like yours. And once it seemed to me the other was sitting at my bedside and told me I should follow him and let myself be crucified. I have never understood that. But it allured me unspeakably. The strange boy appeared to me almost nightly for a time in my dreams. Later he stayed away. And I forgot him."

Melchior became more and more agitated.

"Crucified?" he whispered almost inaudibly, "when was that? Everything in me is thrown into confusion. I can remember nothing anymore."

He stood up and paced restlessly back and forth. Then he sat down and spoke softly to himself:

"The cross is raised... Dawn breaks... The body tears... The blood streams into the world... And one grows anew into the immense... When was that? To whom did that happen?... We must wander, dear Mahr, always wander. We must leave everything. We must be free. We must not rest. I too must set out on my wandering again... I want to go... Will you come along?"

Mahr looked into the old man's eyes. And in a sudden movement he took his hand, kissed it and said:

"I will come along."

"Leave me now," murmured the old man, "come again tomorrow. And prepare yourself for the wandering."

When Mahr had gone, Melchior remained for a while standing thoughtfully in the middle of the room. His aimlessly wandering gaze fell on the oblong wall mirror. And as if under a compulsion, he began to undress completely, stood before the mirror and contemplated himself for a long time.

His body was of flawless beauty and bore no traces yet of age and decay. Only the pale, furrowed face seemed mask-like rigid and the eyes stared motionless and lusterless before them. He paced back and forth before the mirror and examined the play of his limbs.

"Is it myself who looks out there from the silvery surface?" he thought, "what does this aged head want on this young body? I am still strong. I can still go. Mahr will fetch me tomorrow. And we go... When have I waited for departure just as today? Where have I seen his face? His walk? His form? Does he lure me away from here? Am I the one who lures him? I don't know. I only know that we must go. Away, into the distance, into the immense..."

And he dressed himself slowly again and smiled a little mockingly at the adventure he had had with himself before the mirror. From habit he sat down at his desk to work. Then it occurred to him that this no longer made any sense. And anew he felt troubled and oppressed.

He could no longer bear it in the room. And even though it was already late, he left his dwelling and went for the first time, since he had lived in the city, to the coffee house.

The faces he saw there repelled him with their calm, self-assurance, and contentment. Then he was addressed. A colleague greeted him and said:

"So the festival has lured you out too today?"

"What festival?" asked Melchior.

"You don't know that? The centenary celebration!"

"What centenary celebration?"

"The centenary celebration of the great revolution of Stuhlbrestenburg."

Melchior stared into emptiness.

"Revolution of Stuhlbrestenburg?" he said finally, "that was a hundred years ago already?"

"Yes, certainly," answered the colleague, "things looked a bit different in the world back then than today. A dark time, dear colleague, a dark time. Confused, superstitious, depraved, and overwrought. The last attempts of individuals to force the whole under their will. The last romanticism of great deeds and works. The last outbreaks of a mystical intoxication-craving. We can hardly comprehend any of that anymore. We live bright and awake. We know neither slavery nor freedom. We live for each other and with each other. We work no more than is absolutely necessary. And for the rest we are here in the world, that suffices us. People used to be more dissatisfied and more restless."

"Yes, more restless..." said Melchior.

"More restless and more uncertain," the colleague continued, "we no longer know that longing for the unbounded of which there was so much talk back then. We are finite. That feeling of restlessness, that urge out of the unbearableness of daily life, that craving for the absolute—back then it created quite strange fantasy-images which, according to credible reports, were generally taken for reality. It's said that shortly before the revolution mysterious boys appeared throughout the land and caused the greatest confusion everywhere, among both the ruling circles and the revolutionaries. They supposedly possessed the ability to transform their shape at will, it was claimed. Imagine: the irresponsible jests of a high-spirited society—for it could not have been anything else—were taken back then for supernatural events. And then the deranged legend about the events at the court theater where the last king lost his life... You've certainly heard of it?"

"No, dear friend," Melchior interrupted him, "but I am too tired, I must go home, another time!"

And he took his leave quickly and ran out into the street to breathe more freely.

The endlessly long streets with their chains of white arc-lamps on both sides, reflected insubstantially in the rain-wet asphalt, were already deserted when Melchior walked toward his dwelling. The feeling of his own walking, which became ever freer and lighter, filled him with floating pleasure.

"So it is good," he thought, "so I will walk on and on. Ever again along festive streets, white fires as spirit-torches at my side!"

And in walking on he did not notice how he lost consciousness of the present. Still indistinctly, dimly glowing images rose in him, bathed everything around him in pale, milky light, and gradually became clearer and more radiant until he could no longer distinguish them from reality.

The streets widened. The houses grew. The sky circled lower and more rushing. The clouds tore. Great yellow stars glowed hot and near.

His heart beat more violently. Then he believed he heard steps behind him, barely audible, creeping, evil steps. He looked around. No one was to be seen. And yet he could not free himself from the horror that had overcome him.

Only gradually did he regain his former lightness and freedom. Ever stranger, more distant and larger, streets and houses, sky, arc-lamps and stars surrounded him.

Anew he heard steps. But they were not the same ones that had frightened him before. They sounded into the rhythm of his own steps, from all sides he believed he heard invisible wanderers. The blood pulsed more fiery through his veins. Without knowing it, he sang aloud into the night. And from everywhere invisible choirs answered him.

A clock tower struck. He started. His heart stopped. And suddenly fear sat in his throat and would not let him go.

But the singing grew louder. Accompanied by distant fifes, drums, and cymbals, a stamping march approached and swept him along.

And he saw himself riding into a radiant city on a white horse. On all streets sounded music and bright calling. Two youths led his horse by the bridle. On both sides ran in long rows naked boys and youths, wreaths in their hair and around their loins, torches in outstretched arms.

The street opened onto a square surrounded by tall white buildings, above which colored lights floated in the mild night air. The windows and balconies of the palaces were full of veiled women and girls. When the procession had reached the middle of the square, they let their veils fall, stood there naked and threw roses onto the square.

A tall decorated gate opened before Melchior. Hundreds of flags billowed above him. Boys held his stirrups. He dismounted—and suddenly stood alone before his small house door in the empty street. The lamps blinked sad and weary. It was snowing in large flakes.

He could not take another step. His knees swayed. He sank down onto the snow-covered pavement, burrowed his head in the snow and began to weep uncontrollably.

So he lay there until he had no more tears. Then he rose and climbed with heavy feet up the steps to the house door. As he put the key in the lock, he recoiled. The door seemed to warn him and threaten him. The fear was there, which had seized him twice before on the street.

He considered whether he should turn back and wait for morning in the coffee house. But then he imagined the long, deserted streets and an icy shudder ran through him at the thought of the eerie lonely way. Also a sudden leaden tiredness overcame him, luring him ever more strongly into the room.

He overcame his horror, unlocked and quickly closed the door behind him, as if he feared someone invisible might enter the hallway with him. In the stairwell he stopped and listened long, timidly, into the darkness. At last he decided to go upstairs. While climbing he hesitated once more. And before his apartment door he almost turned back again, so strange and terrible it seemed to him in the pale gleam of the streetlamps behind the stairway window.

When he had entered the apartment, he hurried hastily to his room. He struck a match and immediately let it fall again. He felt that someone strange was there.

And now he heard distinctly the breaths of a sleeper. He believed he knew these breaths, but could not recall. It must have been at that time which had sunk from him and which in recent days had knocked ever more admonishingly at his heart.

Once more he let the match flare up and lit a candle.

In the armchair by the fireplace, his back turned to him, sat a man with blond, wavy hair. Melchior stepped closer, shone the light into the sleeper's face and recognized Ulrich von Spät. At the same moment the fog in his memory tore apart. And he knew everything that had been.

### FINAL CHAPTER: THE SNOW-WANDERER

As once in time immemorial he saw his enemy sleeping.

"He is in my power," he thought, "now I am his master. I am awake. He believed me powerlessly sunk and fell asleep. Now the boys shall bind him and tear him apart."

He looked at the sleeper. And again the severe, pure beauty of his divine countenance seized him and sought to bind him in its spell. But he shook off the temptation, stepped back and cried:

"I want to go!"

Nothing stirred.

And he raised his arms and cried once more:

"I want to go!"

But all remained still as before. No one came.

For the third time he spoke the conjuring words. In vain.

Then he let his arms sink and knew he was alone. The boys were in the power of the lords. Ulrich could sleep peacefully.

"It is over," thought Melchior and felt unutterably tired. He looked shyly at Ulrich, who still slept soundly. He was afraid to see his eyes and hear him speak. Cautiously, without undressing, he lay down on his bed and fell asleep at once.

He dreamed he was at the foot of a steep mountain. A rocky path wound upward. He climbed hastily upward through stone deserts. The waning moon faintly lit the way. High on the summit he saw a towered city rising into the stars. The path became ever narrower and more precipitous. At last Melchior reached the first houses. A watchman bowed before him with a composed gesture. On the streets Melchior encountered slowly wandering figures whose faces bore the same expression as that of the watchman. When they passed one another, they looked at each other smiling but spoke no word. They were like the fading shadows of the departed who greeted each other once more before dissolving into the formless void. The same rhythm and beat governed all their movements. None was distinguishable from another.

"It is the city of the glass lords," Melchior said to himself, "from here they silently direct the fates of mankind. The whole world will become like this city. The earth falls silent!"

He searched in vain in all the faces for a desire, a longing, a yearning. Without joy and without pain they wound through one another as in a strict round dance.

A boundless hatred awoke in him and shook him like a fever. His eyes glowered threateningly at those passing by. But they paid him no heed. A barely audible music, gliding steadily as from bodiless light-sources, emanated from them. They seemed not to hear it. Their expressions remained indifferent and unmoved.

Melchior walked up and down the streets and always encountered the same indistinguishable crowds. Now and then a bell struck. Then one detached himself from the ranks and walked slowly away through a pointed archway.

"I will follow them," thought Melchior, "I want to know where they go."

And when the bell struck anew, he followed the departing one out into the open. Again he saw a rocky path winding steeply downward. He entered it and felt how a dark power lured him valleyward. The figure walked faster and faster before him. He quickened his pace. It seemed to glide away as if borne by the wind. He ran after it, gasping for breath.

And suddenly an abyss opened before him, over which the figure floated. He could no longer stop his running, in the next moment he must plunge into the chasm. Then a stream of fire shot like a bridge over the abyss and he ran across the bridge and burned his feet. Thorn-thickets blocked his way and tore his skin. He paid no heed.

"I must follow the emissary," he panted feverishly, "he goes into the world to mankind, to force them into the glass spell. I must awaken them. I must warn them!"

But the path became a rushing water and the figure was no longer to be seen. At last he beheld it already far away in a small boat shooting down the plunging river. And he drew a deep breath and with a cry threw himself into the waves. It grew dark around him. He could see nothing more and only heard the furious roaring of the floods in his ears. A violent blow hurled him ashore.

He found himself alone on an open meadow. Springing up quickly, he wanted to hurry on, but immediately stopped again. Close before him an empty cross rose into the sky. He recognized the meadow on which he had been crucified. It lay desolate and forsaken. Gazing up at the cross, he saw that it was not a cross but a dead tree with bare branches. From afar he heard the sea roaring. Blue darkness lightened. Mists rose and swelled. A crumbling hall of columns enclosed him. Withered flower-garlands wreathed a disheveled bed. No one was to be seen. Melchior's steps on the cracked marble floor echoed hollowly from the countless columns.

He breathed heavily and began to run. Soon the hall lay behind him and a rank primeval forest received him. Between the trunks lay rotting animal carcasses. Not a breath of wind stirred. No sound was to be heard but the distant roaring of the sea.

Then from afar he heard his name called. Who was seeking him? He ran faster. The calling came nearer. The forest thinned. The sea opened before his gaze. And from the shore, bleeding from a thousand wounds, Ulrich walked toward him.

Melchior drew out his knife, crouched and waited. When Ulrich stood before him, he threw himself upon him and quick as lightning carved a cross on his breast.

Ulrich screamed:

"Melchior..."———

And Melchior awoke, looked about with bewildered eyes and saw Ulrich, a burning candle in his hand, standing beside his bed. It was still night.

"The earth is mine," said Ulrich, "you called the boys in vain. They could not hear you. They are only reflections in a mirror. They dissolve into the light."

"I am not yours!" cried Melchior, "I will tear them back again into my world, into the world of longing and confusion, into the transformation from form to form. My will is free."

"I will break your will, as I have broken the will of others!" said Ulrich calmly. "Form is illusion. I annihilate the deception. I tear the veil. I dissolve the play into truth!"

"Then I will the illusion eternally, the deception eternally, the play eternally. I weave the veil anew!" Melchior rose and looked Ulrich firmly in the eye.

"Come with me," said Ulrich, "and I will show you the end of the play."

"The play never ends!"

"Come with me," Ulrich repeated, "and see!"

When they stepped out onto the street, the snowfall had become even denser. A storm drove the flakes into their faces. All outlines blurred in the white whirl.

They walked for over an hour through long streets until they came to a district Melchior did not yet know. A maze of narrow, dark alleyways surrounded them. At a dilapidated house, before whose gate a dim oil lantern burned, Ulrich stopped. Over the entrance, in faded gold letters, blazed the words:

#### *"World Stage Radium"*

"We have arrived," said Ulrich, who had not spoken the whole way, and knocked three times on the gate with the knob of his stick. The wings creaked on their hinges. A distorted, aged dwarf's face peered out.

"You come late," said the dwarf in a creaking voice, "no audience anymore, no one wants to watch. But we're playing the piece to the end. The last act is about to begin."

He fetched from a corner an ancient, dusty stable-lantern, lit it laboriously with trembling fingers, and chuckled:

"The gentlemen shall be well served. Please follow me."

And he walked ahead and led them through dirty, damp corridors with cracked walls, over endless stairs with crumbling steps. At last he tore open a small door in the wallpaper and shrieked:

"Please enter. The last act is about to begin. Much pleasure, gentlemen!"

He fastened the lantern to the wall of the box and withdrew with many bows, grinning. They sat down on two worn upholstered chairs at the balustrade. The auditorium was dark. Only here and there a small lantern burned, and in its glow one saw scattered shadows moving back and forth.

Melchior looked around the box, which was on the third tier, close beside the stage. The walls were covered with light-green wallpaper full of yellowish stains and coming loose from the wall in places. The hanging tatters rustled incessantly in the cold draft that blew through the desolate space. Melchior shivered.

"A good seat," said Ulrich, "one sees the actors from an angle that prevents one from taking their play too tragically."

"What does this matter to us? What am I to see here?" asked Melchior irritably.

"The last act!" replied Ulrich.

A minutes-long shrill bell rang out. At the same time the curtain flew rushing upward.

And Melchior beheld on the stage a street of the same city he had beheld in his dream a few hours before. Again the transparent inhabitants with the unmoved faces wound through one another as in a strict round dance. And this time Melchior knew who they were: he recognized the boys. But in the midst of those wandering he beheld Ulrich, who sat beside him, a second time. The whole round seemed to turn around him.

And Ulrich stood up, sat down on the somewhat raised chair behind Melchior, drew out a large pair of opera glasses, braced his elbows on Melchior's shoulders and looked down over his head at the stage.

Then Ulrich's mirror-image down there raised his arms and one of the dancers moved toward him with measured steps, placed himself beside him and cried:

"Time sinks. Space scatters. Form expires."

It was Fo's voice. Melchior wanted to spring up. But Ulrich's elbows weighed heavily on his shoulders and held him down.

The round on the stage halted. The dancers arranged themselves in pairs. An immense gate towered in the background. From the cracks of its wings light poured forth. All froze in expectation. The last smile on their lips had died. Their eyes were closed as in deep sleep. Ulrich's double too slowly let his eyelids fall.

Melchior felt the pressure of Ulrich's elbows on his shoulders grow weaker. He turned and saw that Ulrich himself had fallen asleep.

Shaking the elbows from his shoulders, he drew breath and conquered the sleep that threatened to fetter him too. He laughed freely and straightened up. His mouth cried strange words that broke from his heart, ringing out into the space.

Then he saw a new figure enter the stage and recognized in it himself. He saw himself down there hurry to Fo and shake him violently. He saw how Fo slowly opened his eyes and sprang up. He heard himself cry:

"He sleeps. Now is the time!"

And they threw themselves with flashing knives upon Ulrich's mirror-image.

At this moment Ulrich sank from the chair in the box and fell lifeless like a log to the floor. At the same time Melchior saw himself on the stage hurrying away with Fo, before the gate had yet opened, which now opened wide into shimmering emptiness to receive the fading sleepers.

A storm-wind seized Melchior and swept him away. Snowflakes struck his face. Pale morning dawned. He found himself alone on a snow-covered country road. Far away the city smoked with haze.

The snowfall became ever thinner and slower. The sun seemed to want to break through the clouds.

Melchior felt his strength leaving him. Sweet exhaustion spread through him. He was so weak he could not take another step. Powerless he let himself fall into the snow and gazed into the distance.

"The circles close," he whispered to himself, "it is accomplished. My shadow has freed your shadow. The enemy is torn apart... Where do you tarry now on the wide earth? Behind the great seas that divide us, my secret calling has awakened you from glass-like sleep. You wander over plains, climb over high mountains, day and night, night and day. Golden ships with reddish sails carry you over bright seas. Swarms of screeching birds swirl around your head. On wild paths you stride toward me, nearer and ever nearer. One day there will be a morning. In earliest dawn a sounding gust of wind will sweep over the earth. And you will step toward me, naked and glowing, the dust of whirling stars in your hair, the reflection of gleaming glaciers on your brow, and will kiss my twitching heart with cool lips. The earth will be silent no more: your word calls from all life, your breath sounds from every body, your love blooms from every heart. The cross will be raised. Those awakened pour their blood into the veins of the world and transform from form to form. The new play begins. Blue melts into fire. Darker grapes press together and await you. See, we rest in breathing bliss. Stillness expands toward you. Step out from foliage-night to us in naked blaze, young flame, singing flame, Lord and Child!"

And he rose once more and stretched his limbs. Staggering he trudged through the snow, along the endless road, until he came to a footpath that rose gently upward.

Then he beheld a drop of blood lying in the snow. He looked more closely and saw that it was a red rose petal. Some steps farther another rose petal lay. Still another and another sparkled forth. The whole path was strewn with them. And in the snow appeared the traces of delicate, bare feet.

Willlessly Melchior followed the traces. They led higher and higher. The mist thickened around him. The earth below vanished from his gaze. Everything was white and became ever whiter and whiter. Only the rose petals glowed blood-red and beckoned.

And he believed he saw, far in the mist, the back of a figure walking before him. He no longer felt his weakness. He knew nothing more of himself. He saw only the figure before him, around which a silver radiance spread that slowly turned golden.

Then the sun broke through. The mists parted abruptly. High on the summit stood Fo in trickling radiance, roses in his hair and around his loins, and spread flaming arms.

The weary wanderer sank to his knees.

"The kingdom," he stammered, "the kingdom without space..."

His pulses tore. His heart burst.
